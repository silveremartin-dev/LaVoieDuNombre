### 1. The Problem: The Merchant's Heavy Cargo

We stand on the river wharves. A sturdy wooden river barge has just docked, laden with sacks of grain.

The dock foreman holds a clay tablet. He must tally the shipments from two separate agricultural estates:
- The Sun Estate brings **47 sacks of wheat**.
- The Moon Estate brings **38 sacks of barley**.

The city governor demands the grand total before nightfall.  
The foreman begins carving small notches on his slate: $1, 2, 3, 4\dots$  
It is far too slow! His vision blurs, the lines cross, and dockworkers shout with mounting impatience:  
*“How many sacks in total? We want to unload the ship!”*

To calculate without error, even with large quantities, ancient merchants did not line up loose stones on the dirt. They utilized an ingenious device: **the counting abacus**.

---

### 2. The Calculating Machine: The Abacus and the Two-Column Box

Let us construct the earliest precursor to the computer! You only need a shoe box and a cardboard partition dividing it into two compartments:

```
                  THE COUNTING BOX
          ┌──────────────────┬──────────────────┐
          │       TENS       │       ONES       │
          │       ( t )      │       ( o )      │
          │                  │                  │
          │  [cork stoppers] │ [pebbles/beads]  │
          └──────────────────┴──────────────────┘
```

Recall the foundational rule discovered in Chapter 2:
- In the **Ones ($o$)** compartment, we only place small single pebbles (1 pebble = 1 sack).
- In the **Tens ($t$)** compartment, we only place cork stoppers (1 stopper = 10 sacks).
- **Absolute Rule:** The moment **10 pebbles** accumulate in the ones compartment, the bin is completely full! We must immediately exchange those 10 pebbles for **1 cork stopper**, which shifts across into the tens compartment.

On grid paper, we draw this exact structure in vertical columns: this is known as **setting up a standard vertical algorithm**.

---

### 3. Vertical Addition Without Regrouping: Perfect Alignment

Let us begin with a gentle addition:
$$23 + 45$$

To set up this calculation on grid paper, follow the golden rule of alignment:

> **🧭 Alignment Rule — Everyone in Their Column!**  
> Write the numbers one directly above the other, aligning vertically:  
> - **Ones under ones** (the right-hand column).  
> - **Tens under tens** (the left-hand column).  
> - Draw a straight horizontal line beneath: this line represents the equal sign $=$.  
> - **ALWAYS begin by adding the ones column first** (from right to left)!

```
          t   o
          2   3      (2 tens and 3 ones)
      +   4   5      (4 tens and 5 ones)
      ──────────
```

1. **Step 1: Add the ones.**  
   In the right-hand column: $3 + 5 = 8$.  
   Write **8** below the line, in the ones column.

2. **Step 2: Add the tens.**  
   In the left-hand column: $2 + 4 = 6$.  
   Write **6** below the line, in the tens column.

```
          t   o
          2   3
      +   4   5
      ──────────
          6   8      The sum is 68!
```

Everything remained orderly: neither column reached 10, no pebbles overflowed.

### 4. Addition with Regrouping: The Traveling Carry

Now let us return to the barge cargo:
$$47 + 38$$

Set up the columns:

```
          t   o
          4   7
      +   3   8
      ──────────
```

1. **Inspect the ones column:**  
   We unite 7 pebbles and 8 pebbles.  
   $$7 + 8 = 15$$  
   **Alert!** 15 is a two-digit number!  
   We cannot write 15 in the ones box, because each column can hold only a single digit from 0 to 9.

2. **The magic trade:**  
   What do 15 pebbles represent?  
   $$15 \text{ ones} = 1 \text{ ten} + 5 \text{ ones}$$  
   - The **5 ones** remain seated at the bottom of the column, below the line.  
   - The **1 brand-new ten** cannot stay in the ones bin: it packs its bag and **travels up to the top of the tens column**!

This traveling ten is called **the carry** (or regrouped ten). We write it as a small superscript numeral circled at the top of the tens column:

```
         (1) <----- The traveling carry!
          t   o
          4   7
      +   3   8
      ──────────
              5      (7 + 8 = 15: write 5, carry 1)
```

3. **Compute the tens column:**  
   Do not forget to include the traveling newcomer!  
   $$1 \text{ (carry)} + 4 + 3 = 8 \text{ tens}$$

```
         (1)
          t   o
          4   7
      +   3   8
      ──────────
          8   5      The total is 85 sacks!
```

> **📐 Definition — The Carry (Regrouping)**  
> In a vertical algorithm, a **carry** is a ten (or hundred) produced by an overflow of the preceding column, which travels to join the top of the next larger place-value column.

> **⚠️ Watch Out! — Forgetting the Carry**  
> The most frequent student mistake is forgetting to add the carry when summing tens! To guarantee success:  
> 1. Record the carry immediately the moment you write down the ones digit.  
> 2. Form the habit of starting the tens addition **with the carry**: “1 + 4 = 5, and 5 + 3 = 8.”

> **🔍 True Story — Blaise Pascal's Calculator (1642)**  
> To assist his father, a royal tax collector who spent long nights adding endless ledgers of figures, 19-year-old French scientist Blaise Pascal invented the first mechanical calculator: **the Pascaline**. Inside, an ingenious sautoir gear mechanism automatically carried over a 1 to the neighboring wheel whenever a dial turned past 9!

### 5. Vertical Subtraction: Unpacking a Ten

Now consider the reverse narrative:  
The barge initially contained **85 sacks**. Merchants sell **38 sacks** on the wharf.  
How many sacks remain on board?  
$$85 - 38$$

Set up the subtraction vertically:

```
          t   o
          8   5
      -   3   8
      ──────────
```

As always, start with the ones column on the far right:  
We have 5 loose pebbles, and we must remove 8:  
$$5 - 8 = \text{ ??}$$  
**That is impossible!** If you have 5 berries in your pocket, you cannot hand 8 to your friends!

What does the merchant do? He does not panic. He looks to the tens column, which holds 8 sealed bundles of 10 sacks.

#### The Decomposing Method (Borrowing / Unpacking):
1. The merchant takes **1 bundle of ten** from the tens store.  
   There were 8 tens: now only **7 tens** remain! (Cross out the 8 and write 7 above).
2. He unties this bundle: it releases **10 fresh ones**.
3. He adds these 10 ones to the 5 already sitting in the ones column:  
   $$10 + 5 = 15 \text{ ones!}$$  
   (Place a small 1 beside the 5 to form 15).

```
          (7)  (15)
           t     o
          \8\   \5\
      -    3     8
      ─────────────
```

4. Now subtraction proceeds effortlessly!  
   - In the ones column: $15 - 8 = \mathbf{7}$.  
   - In the tens column: $7 - 3 = \mathbf{4}$.

```
          (7)  (15)
           t     o
          \8\   \5\
      -    3     8
      ─────────────
           4     7      47 sacks remain!
```

> **💡 Physical Action — Unpacking a Ten**  
> Take 8 matchboxes filled with 10 matches each (80) and 5 loose matches (5).  
> You must remove 8 matches. You cannot draw 8 from the 5 loose ones.  
> You take **one closed matchbox**, open it, and dump its 10 matches onto the table with the 5 loose ones: you now have 15 loose matches and 7 closed boxes. You can remove 8 matches with complete ease!

### 6. The Detective's Secret: Verifying with the Inverse Operation

How does a skilled mathematician confirm that an answer is accurate without waiting for an instructor's red pen?  
By performing **the inverse operation proof**!

- You calculated the subtraction: $85 - 38 = 47$.  
- To verify, execute the reverse: take the 47 remaining sacks and add back the 38 sacks sold ($47 + 38$).  
- If your work was flawless, you must recover **the exact 85 initial sacks**!

```
      Executed subtraction:      Verification addition:
          8 5                         4 7
        - 3 8                       + 3 8
        ─────                       ─────
          4 7                         8 5   <--- Exactly 85 recovered: verified!
```

If the result does not yield 85, a calculation error occurred: you can track down the slip before anyone else spots it!

### 7. Five Step-by-Step Solved Examples

> **Solved Example 1 — Vertical Addition Without Carrying**  
> *Problem:* Calculate $34 + 52$.  
> *Steps:*  
> ```
>       t   o
>       3   4
>   +   5   2
>   ─────────
>       8   6
> ```  
> - Ones: $4 + 2 = 6$.  
> - Tens: $3 + 5 = 8$.  
> *Answer:* $34 + 52 = 86$.

> **Solved Example 2 — Vertical Addition with a Carry**  
> *Problem:* Calculate $56 + 27$.  
> *Steps:*  
> ```
>      (1)
>       t   o
>       5   6
>   +   2   7
>   ─────────
>       8   3
> ```  
> - Ones: $6 + 7 = 13$. Write 3 in ones; carry 1 to tens.  
> - Tens: $1 \text{ (carry)} + 5 + 2 = 8$.  
> *Answer:* $56 + 27 = 83$.

> **Solved Example 3 — Three-Story Addition**  
> *Problem:* Calculate $25 + 18 + 34$.  
> *Steps:*  
> ```
>      (1)
>       t   o
>       2   5
>       1   8
>   +   3   4
>   ─────────
>       7   7
> ```  
> - Ones: $5 + 8 = 13$, and $13 + 4 = 17$. Write 7, carry 1.  
> - Tens: $1 \text{ (carry)} + 2 + 1 + 3 = 7$.  
> *Answer:* $25 + 18 + 34 = 77$.

> **Solved Example 4 — Vertical Subtraction Without Borrowing**  
> *Problem:* Calculate $69 - 25$.  
> *Steps:*  
> ```
>       t   o
>       6   9
>   -   2   5
>   ─────────
>       4   4
> ```  
> - Ones: $9 - 5 = 4$.  
> - Tens: $6 - 2 = 4$.  
> *Answer:* $69 - 25 = 44$.

> **Solved Example 5 — Vertical Subtraction with Decomposing**  
> *Problem:* Calculate $72 - 36$.  
> *Steps:*  
> - Ones: $2 - 6$ is impossible!  
> - Borrow 1 ten from 7: 6 tens remain.  
> - Transfer 10 ones to 2: it becomes 12.  
> ```
>      (6)  (12)
>       t     o
>      \7\   \2\
>   -   3     6
>   ────────────
>       3     6
> ```  
> - Ones: $12 - 6 = 6$.  
> - Tens: $6 - 3 = 3$.  
> *Check by addition:* $36 + 36 = 72$. Flawless!

---

### Key Takeaways

1. To execute vertical operations, align **ones under ones** and **tens under tens**. Compute from right to left.
2. **In addition**, if the sum of ones reaches 10 or greater, write down the ones digit and send the ten as a **carry** to the top of the tens column.
3. Always include the carry when summing the tens column.
4. **In subtraction**, if the top digit is smaller than the bottom digit, **decompose a ten**: the tens column loses 1, and the ones column gains 10.
5. Verify subtractions by adding the difference back to the subtracted amount.

### Solutions

**Exercise 1.**  
- $21 + 35 = \mathbf{56}$.  
- $42 + 16 = \mathbf{58}$.

**Exercise 2.**  
- $38 + 25 = \mathbf{63}$ (carry 1).  
- $47 + 36 = \mathbf{83}$ (carry 1).  
- $59 + 14 = \mathbf{73}$ (carry 1).

**Exercise 3.**  
- $57 - 23 = \mathbf{34}$.  
- $88 - 41 = \mathbf{47}$.  
- $76 - 35 = \mathbf{41}$.

**Exercise 4.**  
The student calculated $8 + 5 = 13$, wrote down 3, but **forgot to add the carried 1** in the tens column! They computed $4 + 2 = 6$ instead of $1 + 4 + 2 = 7$.  
The correct sum is **73**.

**Exercise 5.**  
$24 + 38 = \mathbf{62\text{ liters}}$.

**Exercise 6.**  
- $53 - 28 = \mathbf{25}$ (borrow 1 ten: $13 - 8 = 5$, $4 - 2 = 2$).  
- $61 - 37 = \mathbf{24}$ (borrow 1 ten: $11 - 7 = 4$, $5 - 3 = 2$).  
- $84 - 49 = \mathbf{35}$ (borrow 1 ten: $14 - 9 = 5$, $7 - 4 = 3$).

**Exercise 7.**  
$46 + 39 = 85 \ne 75$. The classmate is mistaken by one ten!  
The true difference is **36** ($36 + 39 = 75$).

**Exercise 8.**  
- $65 + 47 = \mathbf{112}$.  
- $78 + 56 = \mathbf{134}$.  
- $89 + 35 = \mathbf{124}$.

**Exercise 9.**  
$92 - 35 = \mathbf{57\text{ sheep}}$.

**Exercise 10.**  
$28 + 34 + 19 = \mathbf{81\text{ balloons}}$ (carry 2).

**Exercise 11.**  
- Ones: $\star + 4 = 11 \implies \star = \mathbf{7}$.  
- Tens: $1 + 3 + \star = 8 \implies \star = \mathbf{4}$.  
The addition was $37 + 44 = 81$.

**Exercise 12.**  
Borrowing 1 ten turns 4 into 14: $14 - \blacksquare = 7 \implies \blacksquare = \mathbf{7}$.  
Verification: $5 - 2 = 3$. Flawless!

**Exercise 13.**  
In $80 - 36$, 0 cannot yield 6. Borrow 1 ten from 8 (leaving 7). The 10 ones join 0 to make 10.  
$10 - 6 = 4$, and $7 - 3 = 4$. Result is **44**.

**Exercise 14.**  
- Departure: 38.  
- Stop 1: $38 + 15 = 53$; $53 - 7 = 46$.  
- Stop 2: $46 + 12 = 58$; $58 - 9 = 49$.  
**49 passengers** remain.

**Exercise 15.**  
Assigning highest available digits to tens (8 and 5) and ones gives $82 + 5 = 87$ or with duplicate sets $85 + 82 = 167$. Using cards 2, 5, 8 once each: $82 + 5 = 87$ or $85 + 2 = 87$.
