### 1. The Problem: The Feud of the Egyptian Stonecutters

Under the scorching sun on the banks of the Nile, four thousand years ago, hundreds of workers are constructing a grand temple for the pharaoh.

The chief architect traces the blueprints upon a sheet of papyrus:  
*“I demand that each block of stone for the monumental gateway measure exactly **three cubits** in length!”*

Two stonecutters set to work in the quarry:
- The first is named Senned. He is a towering giant over two meters tall, with long, powerful arms like oak trunks.
- The second is named Bak. He is a slender, agile young man with short, delicate arms.

What is a **cubit** to them? It is the distance from the tip of the elbow to the tip of the middle finger (the longest finger of the hand)!

```
   Tip of the elbow                                Tip of the middle finger
         ▼                                                   ▼
         └───────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                             = 1 CUBIT
```

Each mason measures three cubits using his own arm, carves his limestone block from the white bedrock, and hauls it to the construction site.

When the architect attempts to assemble the lintel of the gate, disaster strikes!  
The stone carved by the giant Senned juts out by twenty centimeters, while the block carved by little Bak is far too short and crashes down into the dust!  
The two stonecutters seize each other by the collars, each accusing the other of cheating.

Why did this disaster occur?  
Because **the body of every human being is different**.  
If a giant’s stride is wider than a child’s stride, and if a grandfather’s foot is longer than his grandson’s foot, no one on Earth will ever be able to build a straight house!

### 2. From the Body to the Ruler: The Meter and the Centimeter

To put an end to endless disputes, the pharaoh decreed a royal law:  
*“Henceforth, there shall exist only one single cubit throughout all the land: **the cubit of my own arm**!”*

Royal sculptors carved this length into a rod of polished black granite, preserved within the sacred royal treasury. Wooden copies were crafted for every job site: this was a **standard of measurement**.

Centuries later, during the French Revolution, scientists sought something even grander: a standard belonging to no single monarch, but to all inhabitants of the Earth. They created **the Meter**.

```
   The inch:        The width of an adult's thumb (~ 2 to 3 cm)
   The foot:        The length of a shod foot     (~ 30 cm)
   The pace:        A long adult walking stride   (~ 1 meter)
```

> **📐 Definition — The Meter and the Centimeter**  
> - **The meter ($m$)** is the official base unit of length. It is roughly the width of a bedroom doorway, or the length of a long adult walking stride.  
> - To measure small objects (such as a pencil or an eraser), one meter is divided into 100 equal slices: each slice is called a **centimeter ($cm$)**.  
> - The prefix “centi-” means: *one hundredth*.  
> $$1\text{ meter } (m) = 100\text{ centimeters } (cm)$$

#### How to Measure with Your Ruler Without Falling into the Trap

Pick up your plastic school ruler.

> **⚠️ Watch Out for the Trap! — The plastic edge is not zero!**  
> Many students press the plastic end of their ruler against the start of the object. That is an error! On most rulers, there is a small blank margin before the first graduation line.  
> **To measure accurately, you must align the ZERO ($0$) mark directly with the very start of your object.**  
> Then, you observe which number lands exactly at the end of the object.

```
   Object:       [════════════════════════════]
   Ruler:        |....|....|....|....|....|....|....|
                 0    1    2    3    4    5    6    7
                 ^                             ^
              Align the 0!                  Read: 6 cm!
```

### 3. Mass and Weight: The Roberval Scale and the Kilogram

How heavy is your backpack? How heavy is a bird’s feather?

To weigh objects, we use a two-pan balance perfected by French mathematician and inventor **Gilles Personne de Roberval**.  
On one pan, we place the object to be weighed (for instance, a ripe melon).  
On the other pan, we place calibrated metal **weights** until the pointer rests perfectly vertical in the center.

```
                    Central pointer
                           │
                 ══════════╧══════════
                /                     \
            [ Melon ]              [ 1 kg ]
            
            Perfect equilibrium: the melon weighs 1 kilogram!
```

> **📐 Definition — The Kilogram and the Gram**  
> - For heavy objects, we use the **kilogram ($kg$)**. A bag of flour or a carton of milk weighs approximately $1\text{ kg}$.  
> - For very light objects, we divide a kilogram into 1,000 tiny parts: each part is a **gram ($g$)**.  
> - A paperclip or a kernel of corn weighs roughly $1\text{ g}$.  
> - The prefix “kilo-” comes from Greek and means **thousand**.  
> $$1\text{ kilogram } (kg) = 1{,}000\text{ grams } (g)$$  
> Half a kilogram is $500\text{ g}$.

### 4. Capacities: The Liter

How much water fills your bathtub? How much orange juice is in your cup?  
The volume of liquid that a container can hold is called its **capacity** or capacity volume.

> **📐 Definition — The Liter ($L$)**  
> The standard unit of liquid measurement is the **liter ($L$)**.  
> - A standard bottle of mineral water often holds $1\text{ liter}$ (or $1.5\text{ L}$).  
> - A carton of vegetable soup contains $1\text{ liter}$.  
> - A small soda can contains roughly a third of a liter ($33\text{ cL}$).  
> - A large bucket holds approximately $10\text{ liters}$.  
> - A filled bathtub holds around $150\text{ liters}$.

### 5. Taming the River of Time: From the Hourglass to the Clock

Unlike a rope lying motionless on the floor or a stone resting on a scale, **time never stops**. You cannot touch it with your fingers.

To track time, early humans began by watching the heavens:
1. **Day and Night**: The Earth spins on its axis. When the sun shines, it is day; when it sets, it is night. A full day lasts **24 hours**.  
2. **The Week**: It spans **7 days** (Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday, Saturday, Sunday).  
3. **The Month**: The time required for the Moon to cycle through its phases (roughly 30 or 31 days, except February with 28 or 29).  
4. **The Year**: The time Earth takes to complete one full revolution around the Sun. It contains **12 months** (365 days) and journeys through the four seasons (spring, summer, autumn, winter).

#### Reading an Analog Clock

On a clock face, there are 12 numbers and two hands turning in clockwise rotation:

```
                        12
                   11        1
                10              2
                9        •       3
                 8              4
                    7        5
                         6
```

- **The short hand (small and slow)**: This is the **hour hand**. It takes 12 hours to complete one revolution, gliding slowly from one numeral to the next.  
- **The long hand (tall and fast)**: This is the **minute hand**. It completes a full lap in **60 minutes** (1 hour).

> **🧭 Method — Reading the Clock Step by Step**  
> 1. **On the hour:** When the long hand points straight up at **12**, the short hand indicates the exact hour.  
>    *Example: short hand on 4, long hand on 12 $\rightarrow$ it is 4 o'clock ($4\text{:}00$).*  
> 2. **Half past ($30$ minutes):** When the long hand points straight down at **6**, it has completed half a turn. We say “half past”.  
>    *Example: short hand halfway between 4 and 5, long hand on 6 $\rightarrow$ 4:30.*  
> 3. **Quarter past ($15$ minutes):** Long hand pointing right at **3** $\rightarrow$ quarter past ($4\text{:}15$).  
> 4. **Quarter to ($45$ minutes):** Long hand pointing left at **9** $\rightarrow$ quarter to ($4\text{:}45$).  
>  
> $$1\text{ hour } (h) = 60\text{ minutes } (min)$$  
> $$1\text{ minute } (min) = 60\text{ seconds } (s)$$

### 6. Money: Everyday Currency

To purchase bread from the baker or buy a book, we no longer trade goats or bushels of wheat: we use **currency**.  
In many European countries, the currency is the **Euro (€)**; in other places, the dollar (\$), pound, or yen is used.

- **Coins:**  
  - Copper coins: $1\text{ cent}$, $2\text{ cents}$, $5\text{ cents}$.  
  - Brass coins: $10\text{ cents}$, $20\text{ cents}$, $50\text{ cents}$.  
  - Bimetallic coins: **$1\text{ euro}$** and **$2\text{ euros}$**.  
- **Paper Banknotes:**  
  - $5\text{ €}$, $10\text{ €}$, $20\text{ €}$, $50\text{ €}$, $100\text{ €}$, $200\text{ €}$.

> **📐 Currency Standard — Cents and the Euro**  
> Just like centimeters and meters:  
> $$1\text{ Euro } (€) = 100\text{ cents } (c)$$  
> To make one euro, you need one hundred cents!

### 7. Five Step-by-Step Worked Examples

> **Worked Example 1 — Measuring a Paintbrush with a Ruler**  
> *Problem:* A paintbrush is laid beside a ruler. The handle begins at the $0$ graduation line. The bristle tip touches mark $14$. How long is the brush?  
> *Solution:*  
> Since the start aligns with 0, read the length directly from the end: the brush measures **$14\text{ cm}$**.  
> *(If it started at mark 2 and ended at mark 14, it would measure $14 - 2 = 12\text{ cm}$.)*

> **Worked Example 2 — Comparing Lengths in Meters and Centimeters**  
> *Problem:* A blue ribbon measures $1\text{ m } 25\text{ cm}$. A red ribbon measures $140\text{ cm}$. Which ribbon is longer?  
> *Method:*  
> Convert to the same unit (centimeters):  
> - Blue ribbon: $1\text{ m} = 100\text{ cm}$, so $1\text{ m } 25\text{ cm} = 100 + 25 = 125\text{ cm}$.  
> - Red ribbon: $140\text{ cm}$.  
> Since $140 > 125$, the **red ribbon** is longer.

> **Worked Example 3 — Balancing a Basket on a Roberval Scale**  
> *Problem:* A bag of apples is placed on the left pan of a balance scale. On the right pan, we place a $1\text{ kg}$ weight, a $500\text{ g}$ weight, and two $100\text{ g}$ weights. The scale balances perfectly. What is the mass of the bag of apples?  
> *Solution:*  
> Sum the weights:  
> - $1\text{ kg} = 1{,}000\text{ g}$.  
> - Remaining weights: $500 + 100 + 100 = 700\text{ g}$.  
> - Total: $1{,}000 + 700 = 1{,}700\text{ g}$, which is **$1\text{ kg } 700\text{ g}$**.

> **Worked Example 4 — Duration of a Cartoon**  
> *Problem:* A cartoon starts at $14\text{:}15$ and ends at $15\text{:}15$. How long did it last?  
> *Solution:*  
> The minutes match ($15\text{ min}$).  
> Look at the hours: from 14:00 to 15:00, exactly **$1\text{ hour}$** (60 minutes) elapsed.

> **Worked Example 5 — Making Change at the Bakery**  
> *Problem:* A loaf of bread costs $1\text{ € } 20\text{ c}$. You hand the baker a $2\text{ €}$ coin. How much change will you receive?  
> *Method:*  
> Convert to cents:  
> - $2\text{ €} = 200\text{ cents}$.  
> - Bread cost: $1\text{ € } 20\text{ c} = 120\text{ cents}$.  
> - Subtract: $200 - 120 = 80\text{ cents}$.  
> You receive **$80\text{ cents}$** in change (e.g. a 50c, 20c, and 10c coin).

---

### Key Takeaways

1. To avoid misunderstandings, everyone uses **standardized units**:  
   - Length: **meter ($m$)** and **centimeter ($cm$)** ($1\text{ m} = 100\text{ cm}$).  
   - Mass: **kilogram ($kg$)** and **gram ($g$)** ($1\text{ kg} = 1{,}000\text{ g}$).  
   - Capacity: **liter ($L$)**.  
2. When measuring with a ruler, always align the **$0$** mark with the start of the object.  
3. Time is structured into days (24 h), weeks (7 d), months (30 or 31 d), and years (12 mo).  
4. On a clock: short hand indicates **hours**, long hand indicates **minutes**. $1\text{ h} = 60\text{ min}$.  
5. $1\text{ euro} = 100\text{ cents}$.
