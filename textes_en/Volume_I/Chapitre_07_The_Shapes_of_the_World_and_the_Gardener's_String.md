### 1. The Problem: The Queen's Flowerbed

Long ago, in a splendid kingdom of the Orient, the queen summoned her head gardener:

The gardener bowed low. Yet upon stepping onto the vast stretch of bare earth, he was deeply troubled:

To accomplish this masterpiece, the gardener relied on the simplest, most brilliant tools in the history of geometry: **a piece of string, two wooden pegs, and a folded sheet of paper**.

### 2. Plane Figures: Polygons and Their Secrets

A flat (or plane) figure is a closed drawing that can be traced on a sheet of paper or on the ground without rising up into the air.

When a plane figure is bounded solely by straight line segments (straight sides drawn with a ruler), it is called a **polygon** (from the Greek *polus*, many, and *gônia*, angle or corner).

```
         THE SQUARE               THE RECTANGLE                THE TRIANGLE
      ┌────────────┐            ┌──────────────────┐               ▲
      │            │            │                  │              / \
      │            │            │                  │             /   \
      └────────────┘            └──────────────────┘            └─────┘
     4 equal sides             Opposite sides equal             3 sides
     4 right angles            4 right angles                   3 vertices
```

#### 1. The Square
- It has **4 sides** that measure **exactly the same length**.
- It has **4 right angles** (crisp corners like the corner of a sheet of paper).
- It has **4 vertices** (pointed corners).

#### 2. The Rectangle
- It has **4 sides**, but they are not all equal: it has 2 long lengths and 2 short widths.
- Opposite sides (the sides facing each other) are equal in length.
- It has **4 right angles** and **4 vertices**.

#### 3. The Triangle
- It has **3 straight sides** and **3 vertices** (three angles).
- Its name means: “three angles”.

### 3. The Gardener's String: Tracing a Perfect Circle

Unlike the square or the triangle, **the circle has no straight sides and no corners**! It is a closed, beautifully harmonious curved line.

How did the gardener trace the queen's great round pool?

```
                            Taut string
                 Central ──────────────────── Marking
                   peg                          peg
                   (O)                        (Chalk)
                    │                            │
                    ▼                            ▼
                    •════════════════════════════✎
                     \                          /
                      \                        /
                       \   Full circular turn /
                        \                    /
                         •──────────────────•
```

1. He drives a sturdy **wooden peg straight into the ground at the center** of the future pool (this is the **center** of the circle).  
2. He ties a strong cord securely to this peg.  
3. To the other end of the cord, he fastens a second pointed peg (or a piece of chalk).  
4. He pulls the string until it is **completely taut**.  
5. Keeping the string tight without ever letting it slacken, he walks in a full circle around the center peg, letting the point scratch into the earth.

When he completes the loop, he has traced a **flawless circle**!

> **📐 Definition — Circle, Radius, and Diameter**  
> - A **circle** is the collection of all points located **at the exact same distance** from a central point called the **center**.  
> - The length of the taut string is called the **radius** of the circle.  
> - The straight line passing through the center from one edge to the opposite edge is called the **diameter**. The diameter is always twice the radius ($2 \times \text{radius}$).

### 4. The Right-Angle Gauge: Crafting Your Magic Set Square

To verify that a wall rises vertically or that a table has square corners, carpenters use a triangular tool made of wood or metal: a **set square**.  
Yet you can craft an infallible set square in ten seconds using any scrap of torn paper!

> **🖐️ Workshop — Make Your Own Folded-Paper Set Square**  
> 1. Take any scrap piece of paper, even one with a jagged, crooked edge.  
> 2. Fold it in half once, pressing firmly along the fold with your thumbnail. You now have a perfectly straight edge.  
> 3. Now, fold the paper in half a second time, **bringing the first straight crease directly onto itself**, edge to edge!  
> 4. Crease it firmly.  
> 5. The corner formed by these two folds is a **flawless right angle of $90^\circ$**!  
> Place this magical corner against the corner of your notebook, your desk, or a door frame: it fits to the millimeter!

```
   1. Crooked scrap       2. First straight fold    3. Fold crease onto crease!
        ╭────╮                 ┌────────────┐               ┌─────┐
        │    │        ====>    │            │     ====>     │     │ <--- Right
        ╰────╯                 └────────────┘               └─────┘      angle!
```

### 5. Solids: The World in Three Dimensions

On paper, figures are flat. But in the physical world, objects possess thickness and depth: they take up volume in space. We call them **solids**.

```
          THE CUBE              RECTANGULAR PRISM              CYLINDER
          ┌───────┐                ┌───────────────┐              ╭─────╮
         /       /│               /               /│             │     │
        ┌───────┐ │              ┌───────────────┐ │             │     │
        │       │ │              │               │ │             │     │
        │       │ │              │               │ │             ╰─────╯
        │       │/               │               │/
        └───────┘                └───────────────┘
```

Let us learn the vocabulary of master builders:
- A **face**: a flat wall of the solid (the smooth surface upon which it can rest).  
- An **edge**: the straight line segment where two faces meet (the sharp boundary).  
- A **vertex**: the sharp corner point where several edges meet.

#### The Cube (like a game die)
- It has **6 square faces**, all strictly identical.  
- It has **12 edges** of equal length.  
- It has **8 sharp vertices**.

#### The Rectangular Prism (like a shoebox or brick)
- It has **6 rectangular faces** (which may sometimes include 2 square faces).  
- It has **12 edges** and **8 vertices**.

#### The Sphere (like a marble, an orange, or a soccer ball)
- It has no flat faces, no straight edges, and no vertices. It rolls freely in every direction!

#### The Cylinder (like a paper towel roll or soup can)
- It has **2 circular flat faces** (top and bottom disks) and a curved side that lets it roll in a straight line.

### 6. The Magic Mirror: The Axis of Symmetry

Take a sheet of white paper. Place a drop of blue ink or watercolor in the center.  
Fold the sheet in half along a straight line immediately, press firmly with the palm of your hand, and unfold:

```
                   Fold line (Axis of Symmetry)
                                │
                        \       │       /
                       ( o )    │    ( o )
                       / | \    │    / | \
                      /  |  \   │   /  |  \
                     (   |   )  │  (   |   )
                                │
                        Left    │    Right
                        wing    │    wing
```

You behold a magnificent **butterfly**!  
The right wing is the identical reflection of the left wing.  
If you refold the sheet, the two wings will overlap **exactly**, stroke for stroke, point for point.

> **📐 Definition — Axis of Symmetry**  
> An **axis of symmetry** is a straight line that divides a figure into two halves that match up perfectly when folded.  
> It mirrors what you would see if a flat mirror were placed upright along that line!

Countless things in nature display an axis of symmetry: your own face, a tree leaf, a ladybug, or a snowflake.

### 7. Grids and the Little Robot's Code

To dispatch an apprentice to plant seeds at an exact spot in the garden, the master divided the ground into regular squares: **a grid**.

```
       A       B       C       D
     ┌───┬───┬───┬───┐
   3 │   │   │ ★ │   │   <--- The star is in square (C, 3)
     ├───┼───┼───┼───┤
   2 │   │ 🤖│   │   │   <--- The robot is in square (B, 2)
     ├───┼───┼───┼───┤
   1 │   │   │   │   │
     └───┴───┴───┴───┘
```

To locate any square, we provide its address with two coordinates:
1. First the **column** (the letter: A, B, C, D).  
2. Next the **row** (the number: 1, 2, 3).  
*Example:* The robot is in square **B2**. The golden star shines in square **C3**.

#### Coding Movement with Arrows:
To navigate the robot from square **B2** to the star at **C3**, we write a program using directional arrows:
- $\rightarrow$: move one square to the right.  
- $\uparrow$: move one square up.  
- $\leftarrow$: move one square to the left.  
- $\downarrow$: move one square down.

The shortest path from B2 to C3 is:
$$\rightarrow \quad \uparrow$$
(One step right to land on C2, then one step up to reach C3!)

### 8. Five Step-by-Step Worked Examples

> **Worked Example 1 — Distinguishing a Square from Rectangles**  
> *Problem:* Consider three shapes, each with 4 right angles:  
> - Figure A: width 5 cm, length 8 cm.  
> - Figure B: 4 sides, each measuring 6 cm.  
> - Figure C: width 3 cm, length 7 cm.  
> Which figure is a square?  
> *Solution:*  
> A square must have **4 equal sides** in addition to 4 right angles.  
> Only **Figure B** has 4 equal sides (6 cm). Figures A and C are non-square rectangles.

> **Worked Example 2 — Calculating Diameter from Radius**  
> *Problem:* The gardener staked a 4-meter taut cord from the central peg to outline his pool. What is the total diameter of the pool?  
> *Solution:*  
> The radius is $4\text{ m}$.  
> The diameter spans straight through the center from edge to edge, equal to twice the radius:  
> $$\text{Diameter} = 4\text{ m} + 4\text{ m} = 2 \times 4\text{ m} = \mathbf{8\text{ meters}}$$

> **Worked Example 3 — Counting Features of a Solid**  
> *Problem:* Look at a closed cardboard tissue box (a rectangular prism). How many stickers would you need to put one sticker on every vertex?  
> *Solution:*  
> Vertices are the sharp corners.  
> There are 4 vertices on the top face and 4 on the bottom face:  
> $$4 + 4 = \mathbf{8\text{ vertices}}$$  
> You would need **8 stickers**.

> **Worked Example 4 — Finding Axes of Symmetry of a Rectangle**  
> *Problem:* How many axes of symmetry does an oblong (non-square) rectangle possess?  
> *Practical Method:*  
> Take a sheet of A4 printer paper.  
> 1. Fold it in half horizontally: the two halves align. That is the first axis!  
> 2. Fold it in half vertically: the two halves align. That is the second axis!  
> 3. *What about the diagonal (corner to corner)?* Try folding corner to opposite corner: the edges jut out and do not match! The diagonal of an oblong rectangle is **not** an axis of symmetry.  
> *Conclusion:* A non-square rectangle has **2 axes of symmetry**.

> **Worked Example 5 — Moving on a Grid**  
> *Problem:* A treasure is located at square **D4**. A pirate starts at **A1**.  
> Write the shortest directional code to find the treasure.  
> *Solution:*  
> - From column A to column D: 3 steps right ($A \rightarrow B \rightarrow C \rightarrow D$): $\rightarrow \rightarrow \rightarrow$.  
> - From row 1 to row 4: 3 steps up ($1 \rightarrow 2 \rightarrow 3 \rightarrow 4$): $\uparrow \uparrow \uparrow$.  
> *Complete Program:* $\rightarrow \quad \rightarrow \quad \rightarrow \quad \uparrow \quad \uparrow \quad \uparrow$.

### Key Takeaways

1. **A square** has 4 equal sides and 4 right angles. **A rectangle** has equal opposite sides and 4 right angles. **A triangle** has 3 sides and 3 vertices.  
2. **A circle** is the set of points equidistant from the center. This distance is the **radius**; the **diameter** is twice the radius.  
3. A **right angle ($90^\circ$)** is verified with a set square or double-folded paper.  
4. Solids have **faces** (flat surfaces), **edges** (meeting lines), and **vertices** (corner points).  
   - Cube and rectangular prism: **6 faces**, **12 edges**, **8 vertices**.  
5. A figure has an **axis of symmetry** if its two halves overlap perfectly when folded.  
6. On a grid, a cell is identified by its **column** (letter) and **row** (number). Paths are coded using directional arrows.
