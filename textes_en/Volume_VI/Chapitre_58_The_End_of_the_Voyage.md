### 2. The Guiding Thread: Embodiment and Survival

Why did humanity invent mathematics?
Not to torment schoolchildren, but to **survive**.

1. **The Pebble (*Calculus*) and the Herd (Vol. I):**
   The Neolithic shepherd did not need names for numbers up to 300. He only needed to place a pebble into a leather pouch each time a sheep exited the fold in the morning, and remove one each time a sheep returned in the evening. If pebbles remained in the pouch at twilight, wolves were on the prowl. Computation was born of a physical bijection between stones and beasts.

2. **The Knotted Rope of the Nile Surveyors (Vol. III):**
   Every summer, the flooding of the Nile wiped out field boundaries across Egypt. To collect taxes and feed cities, surveyors had to redraw exact rectangles on dried silt. They knotted a rope into 13 equidistant knots ($3+4+5=12$ intervals) to form a right triangle: Pythagoras was not a classroom theorem; it was the guarantor of fiscal peace and agricultural survival.

3. **The Caravel and the Logarithm (Vol. VI, Chap. 48):**
   Out on the open ocean, navigators computing position from the stars had to multiply 8-digit numbers to correct for drift. A single carry mistake wrecked the ship on coastal reefs. By inventing the logarithm that turns multiplications into additions ($\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b$), John Napier did not merely invent a function: he saved thousands of sailors' lives and doubled the working lifetime of astronomers.

4. **Galileo's Cannon and Leibniz's Derivative (Vol. V & VI):**
   How do you target a moving object? The speed of a cannonball is not constant: gravity drags it down, air resistance slows it down. To compute what changes at each millisecond, one had to invent the infinitesimal rate of change $\frac{dy}{dx}$: calculus was born of the physics of motion.

5. **Network Secrets and the RSA Padlock (Vol. VI, Chap. 56):**
   In a world where every banking transaction, medical record, and vote travels as invisible electromagnetic waves through the air, how do we prevent theft and chaos? By locking data behind the product of two giant prime numbers that only the keyholder can factor.

Every equation you have learned carries the sweat, ingenuity, and urgency of generations of human beings who refused to submit blindly to the world, choosing instead to comprehend it.

### 3. The Three Great Unifying Bridges

At first, mathematics appears segregated into watertight compartments: geometry on one side with rulers and compasses; algebra on another with symbols $x$ and $y$; calculus elsewhere with limits and integrals; and probability with dice and playing cards.
Yet as the mind matures, the partitions crumble. Mathematics is not an archipelago of disconnected islands: it is a **single continent spanned by colossal bridges**.


#### Bridge 1: Algebra and Geometry
- In 1637, **René Descartes** paired every point in space with a coordinate pair $(x, y)$. Suddenly, a geometric line became an equation $ax + by + c = 0$, and a circle became $x^2 + y^2 = R^2$. Solving a geometric dilemma reduced to solving an algebraic system!
- In 1806, **Argand** showed that the imaginary unit $i$ is a geometric quarter-turn: the algebra of complex numbers governs planar rotations and similarities.
- **Matrices** are nothing other than linear transformations of space: stretching, rotating, projecting, and shearing.

#### Bridge 2: The Discrete and the Continuous
- A sequence $(u_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ is a function defined on isolated integers (the discrete world of pebbles). A function $f(x)$ is defined across a continuous interval of real numbers (the world of uninterrupted time and space).
- The bridge? The **limit**. As the step size approaches 0, a finite sum of rectangles transforms into a continuous integral $\int_a^b f(x)dx$ (Riemann).
- As the number of coin tosses approaches infinity, the discrete binomial distribution smooths into the bell curve of the continuous normal distribution (de Moivre-Laplace).

#### Bridge 3: The Local and the Global
This is the most breathtaking bridge of all:
- The **derivative** $f'(x)$ is purely **local**: it inspects only what happens in an infinitesimal neighborhood around the point $x$ (instantaneous speed on a speedometer at one exact moment).
- The **integral** $\int_a^b f(t) dt$ is purely **global**: it accumulates area across the entire interval $[a, b]$ (total distance traveled from start to finish).
- And what miracle unites these two realms?
  The **Fundamental Theorem of Calculus** (Chapter 50):
  $$\int_a^b f'(t) dt = f(b) - f(a)$$
  To discover the global change of a phenomenon between the beginning and the end of the Universe, one simply integrates all its local infinitesimal changes!

### 4. Eugene Wigner's Enigma: Why Does Math Work So Well?

In 1960, Nobel laureate in physics **Eugene Wigner** published a celebrated essay entitled: *“The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences.”*
Wigner posed a haunting question:
Why do concepts created by mathematicians for purely aesthetic pleasure, with zero application in mind, turn out decades or centuries later to be the exact language describing the fundamental laws of the cosmos?

- When **Apollonius of Perga** studied the conic sections (ellipses, parabolas, hyperbolas) in 200 BCE Alexandria, it was out of pure geometric curiosity. Eighteen centuries later, **Johannes Kepler** discovered that the planets in our solar system trace out exact ellipses with the Sun at one focus!
- When **Bernhard Riemann** invented the geometry of curved higher-dimensional spaces in 1854 as a pure mathematical challenge, no one imagined a practical purpose. Sixty years later, **Albert Einstein** seized upon it to formulate General Relativity: gravitation is not a mysterious pull, but the Riemannian curvature of spacetime warped by stellar mass!
- When **Paul Dirac** formulated the relativistic quantum equation of the electron in 1928, the algebra demanded negative-energy solutions. Instead of dismissing them as artifacts of calculation, Dirac trusted the mathematical symmetry and postulated the existence of an antimatter mirror particle with the same mass and opposite charge: the positron. Four years later, Carl Anderson discovered antimatter in a cloud chamber!

The physical world is not merely describable by mathematics: **mathematics appears to be the underlying scaffolding of reality.**

1. **Abstract Linear Algebra:**
   In high school, you manipulated vectors with 2 or 3 coordinates. In higher education, you will explore general **vector spaces**. Functions, sequences, polynomials, and audio signals are all vectors! One can add two functions, multiply them by scalars, and compute their dimension (which can be infinite, as in the Hilbert spaces of quantum mechanics).

2. **General Topology:**
   What does continuity mean when there is no longer a coordinate grid or a real number line? Topology defines proximity, compactness, and connectedness using collections of subsets called “open sets.” It is the study of properties preserved under continuous stretching and bending without tearing.

3. **Complex Analysis (Holomorphic Functions):**
   What happens when we differentiate a function not of a real variable, but of a complex variable $z \in \mathbb{C}$? An absolute wonder occurs: if a complex function is differentiable just once, it is automatically infinitely differentiable and can be expanded as a power series! This is the magic of Augustin-Louis Cauchy's contour integrals and the residue theorem.

4. **Measure Theory and Lebesgue Integration:**
   The Riemann integral has limits: functions oscillating too erratically cannot be integrated. At the dawn of the 20th century, Henri Lebesgue rethought integration by partitioning the $y$-axis rather than the $x$-axis. This integral underpins the modern theory of continuous probability formalized by Andrey Kolmogorov.

5. **Foundational Logic and Gödel's Incompleteness (1931):**
   Can everything in mathematics be proven? In 1931, at age 25, Kurt Gödel published his landmark **Incompleteness Theorems**: in any consistent formal axiomatic system capable of arithmetic, there exist true statements that the system cannot prove using its own rules. Mathematics is not a locked room: it is an infinite, inexhaustible territory forever open to discovery.

### What to Remember from High School

1. **The Derivative $f'(a)$:**
   Slope of the tangent line, instantaneous rate of change. Governs variations, extrema, and convexity.

2. **The Integral $\int_a^b f(x)dx$:**
   Area under the curve, continuous summation. Linked to differentiation by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus: $\int_a^b f(t)dt = F(b) - F(a)$.

3. **The Exponential $e^x$ and the Logarithm $\ln x$:**
   The function identical to its own derivative ($y' = y$) and its inverse. They govern biological growth and physical scaling laws.

4. **Vectors and the Dot Product:**
   $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = \|\vec{u}\| \|\vec{v}\| \cos\theta = xx' + yy' + zz'$. The master tool for orthogonality and projections.

5. **Complex Numbers $\mathbb{C}$:**
   $z = a + ib = r e^{i\theta}$, with $i^2 = -1$. The seamless synthesis of algebra and planar rotations.

6. **Combinatorics and Probability:**
   $\binom{n}{k}$, the binomial distribution $\mathcal{B}(n, p)$, and the Law of Large Numbers: individual randomness surrenders to collective order.

7. **Arithmetic and Matrices:**
   Congruences modulo $n$, Bézout's identity, graphs and Markov chains $P_{n+1} = P_n \times T$.

#### Level ★★ (Synthesis Problems for High School Exit and Competitions)

**Exercise 8. The Euler-Mascheroni Constant $\gamma$.**
Let $H_n = \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{k} = 1 + \frac{1}{2} + \dots + \frac{1}{n}$ denote the harmonic sum.
1. By comparing $\frac{1}{k}$ with $\int_{k}^{k+1} \frac{1}{t} dt$ and $\int_{k-1}^k \frac{1}{t} dt$, prove that:
   $$\ln(n+1) \leq H_n \leq 1 + \ln(n)$$
2. Deduce $\lim_{n \to +\infty} H_n$.
3. Let $u_n = H_n - \ln(n)$. Prove that the sequence $(u_n)$ is non-increasing and bounded below by 0. Deduce that it converges to a finite constant $\gamma \approx 0.5772$ (Euler's constant).

**Exercise 9. Wallis Integrals and Stirling's Formula.**
For all $n \in \mathbb{N}$, define $W_n = \int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \sin^n(t) dt$.
1. Compute $W_0$ and $W_1$.
2. Using integration by parts, establish the recurrence relation:
   $$W_n = \frac{n-1}{n} W_{n-2} \quad (n \geq 2)$$
3. Prove that $(W_n)$ is strictly decreasing and that $\lim_{n \to +\infty} \frac{W_{n+1}}{W_n} = 1$.
4. Deduce John Wallis's infinite product formula:
   $$\lim_{n \to +\infty} \frac{1}{n} \left(\frac{2 \times 4 \times 6 \times \dots \times 2n}{1 \times 3 \times 5 \times \dots \times (2n-1)}\right)^2 = \pi$$

**Exercise 10. Fourier Integral and Parity.**
Compute the integral $\int_0^\pi x \cos(nx) dx$ for $n \in \mathbb{N}^*$ using integration by parts.

**Exercise 11. Solving the Logistic Differential Equation.**
Consider the Verhulst differential equation modeling bounded population growth:
$$y' = a y \left(1 - \frac{y}{K}\right) \quad (a > 0, K > 0)$$
Using the substitution $z = \frac{1}{y}$, solve this equation and sketch the shape of the resulting sigmoid curve.

**Exercise 12. Mersenne Numbers and Euclid's Perfect Numbers.**
An integer $N$ is called perfect if it equals the sum of its proper divisors (for example, $6 = 1 + 2 + 3$).
Prove Euclid's theorem: if $2^p - 1$ is prime, then the integer $N = 2^{p-1}(2^p - 1)$ is an even perfect number.

**Exercise 13. The St. Petersburg Paradox.**
A casino offers a game: a fair coin is tossed until heads appears for the first time. If heads appears on the $k$-th toss, the player wins $2^k$ euros.
1. Compute the theoretical expected monetary value of this game.
2. How much would you be willing to pay to play one round? Discuss the paradox.

**Exercise 14. The Vandermonde Determinant.**
Compute the $3 \times 3$ determinant:
$$V(a, b, c) = \det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & a & a^2 \\ 1 & b & b^2 \\ 1 & c & c^2 \end{pmatrix}$$
Show that it equals $(b - a)(c - a)(c - b)$. Why does this determinant guarantee the uniqueness of the quadratic interpolation polynomial passing through 3 distinct points?

**Exercise 15. The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra via Contradiction.**
Explain why the modulus of a polynomial $|P(z)|$ must attain its global minimum on the complex plane, and why this minimum cannot be strictly positive (d'Alembert's lemma).

#### Level ★★★ (Grand Challenges Preparing for Higher Education)

**Exercise 16. The Basel Problem $\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6}$.**
Consider the polynomial $P_m(X) = \sum_{k=0}^m \binom{2m+1}{2k+1} (-1)^k X^{m-k}$.
Using the roots of the cotangent function, establish Euler's celebrated proof showing that:
$$\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{n^2} = 1 + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{9} + \frac{1}{16} + \dots = \frac{\pi^2}{6}$$

**Exercise 17. The Spectral Theorem for Symmetric Matrices.**
Prove that every real $2 \times 2$ symmetric matrix has two real eigenvalues and can be diagonalized in an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors.

**Exercise 18. The Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality.**
For any real numbers $x_1, \dots, x_n$ and $y_1, \dots, y_n$, prove by examining the sign of the quadratic polynomial $P(t) = \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i t + y_i)^2$ that:
$$\left(\sum_{i=1}^n x_i y_i\right)^2 \leq \left(\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2\right) \left(\sum_{i=1}^n y_i^2\right)$$
Deduce the integral Cauchy-Schwarz inequality: $\left(\int_a^b f(t)g(t) dt\right)^2 \leq \left(\int_a^b f(t)^2 dt\right) \left(\int_a^b g(t)^2 dt\right)$.

**Exercise 19. Banach Fixed-Point Theorem (Contraction Mapping Theorem).**
Let $f : [a, b] \to [a, b]$ be a $k$-contraction (there exists $k \in (0, 1)$ such that $|f(x) - f(y)| \leq k|x - y|$ for all $x, y$).
Prove that $f$ has a **unique fixed point** $\alpha \in [a, b]$, and that for any $u_0 \in [a, b]$, the sequence $u_{n+1} = f(u_n)$ converges to $\alpha$ with geometric rate $|u_n - \alpha| \leq \frac{k^n}{1-k} |u_1 - u_0|$.

**Exercise 20. Central Limit Theorem (Conceptual Overview).**
Explain why the sum of a large number of independent random variables with small, cumulative effects always tends toward a Gaussian normal distribution, and why this universal bell curve appears in biological measurements, astronomical observation errors, electrical thermal noise, and financial fluctuations.

**Exercise 21. Euler's Polyhedral Formula: $V - E + F = 2$.**
Verify this formula for the 5 Platonic solids (Tetrahedron, Cube, Octahedron, Dodecahedron, Icosahedron). Outline the proof via planar projection and edge contraction. Why is the number 2 the foundational topological invariant (Euler characteristic of the sphere)?

2. Why has this deceptively simple problem resisted the greatest mathematicians and supercomputers since 1937? Reflect on the boundaries of mathematical knowledge.

### Detailed Solutions

**Solution 1.**
$f'(x) = 2x e^{-x} + x^2(-e^{-x}) = x(2 - x)e^{-x}$.
Since $e^{-x} > 0$, the sign of $f'(x)$ matches that of $x(2 - x)$: negative on $(-\infty, 0)$, positive on $(0, 2)$, negative on $(2, +\infty)$.
Limits: as $x \to -\infty$, $\lim x^2 e^{-x} = +\infty$. As $x \to +\infty$, by growth hierarchy, $\lim x^2 e^{-x} = 0$.
The absolute maximum occurs at $x = 2$, with $f(2) = 4 e^{-2} = \frac{4}{e^2} \approx 0.5413$.

**Solution 2.**
Recognize the form $\frac{u'(x)}{u(x)}$ with $u(x) = x^2 + 1 > 0$ and $u'(x) = 2x$.
$I = [\ln(x^2 + 1)]_0^1 = \ln(1^2 + 1) - \ln(0^2 + 1) = \ln(2) - \ln(1) = \ln 2$.

**Solution 3.**
$\Delta = (-2\sqrt{3})^2 - 4(1)(4) = 12 - 16 = -4 = (2i)^2$.
$z_1 = \frac{2\sqrt{3} - 2i}{2} = \sqrt{3} - i = 2\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} - \frac{1}{2}i\right) = 2 e^{-i\frac{\pi}{6}}$.
$z_2 = \frac{2\sqrt{3} + 2i}{2} = \sqrt{3} + i = 2 e^{i\frac{\pi}{6}}$.

**Solution 4.**
We know $S_n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$. We wish to prove $\mathcal{P}(n): C_n = \left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{n^2(n+1)^2}{4}$.
- For $n = 1$: $C_1 = 1^3 = 1$ and $\frac{1^2 \times 2^2}{4} = 1$. Holds.
- Inductive step: $C_{n+1} = C_n + (n+1)^3 = \frac{n^2(n+1)^2}{4} + (n+1)^3 = (n+1)^2 \left[\frac{n^2}{4} + n + 1\right] = (n+1)^2 \left[\frac{n^2 + 4n + 4}{4}\right] = \frac{(n+1)^2(n+2)^2}{4}$. $\mathcal{P}(n+1)$ holds.

**Solution 5.**
Total 5-card hands: $\binom{32}{5} = 201,376$.
To get exactly 1 Ace: choose 1 Ace from 4 ($\binom{4}{1} = 4$) and 4 non-Ace cards from 28 ($\binom{28}{4} = 20,475$).
Total favorable outcomes: $4 \times 20,475 = 81,900$.
Probability: $P = \frac{81,900}{201,376} \approx 0.4067$ (about $40.7\%$).

**Solution 6.**
$\det(A) = 7(1) - 3(2) = 1 \neq 0$.
$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{1}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -3 \\ -2 & 7 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -3 \\ -2 & 7 \end{pmatrix}$.
The system is written $A \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 17 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}$.
$\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -3 \\ -2 & 7 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 17 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 17 - 15 \\ -34 + 35 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Thus $x = 2, y = 1$.

**Solution 7.**
11 is prime, $\gcd(5, 11) = 1$. By Fermat's Little Theorem, $5^{10} \equiv 1 \pmod{11}$.
$2026 = 10 \times 202 + 6$.
$5^{2026} = (5^{10})^{202} \times 5^6 \equiv 1 \times 5^6 \pmod{11}$.
$5^2 = 25 \equiv 3 \pmod{11}$.
$5^6 = (5^2)^3 \equiv 3^3 = 27 = 11 \times 2 + 5 \equiv 5 \pmod{11}$. The remainder is **5**.

**Solution 8.**
1. For $t \in [k, k+1]$, $\frac{1}{k+1} \leq \frac{1}{t} \leq \frac{1}{k}$. Integrating over $[k, k+1]$ of unit length: $\frac{1}{k+1} \leq \int_k^{k+1} \frac{1}{t} dt \leq \frac{1}{k}$.
   Summing from $k = 1$ to $n$ yields the inequalities bounding $H_n$.
2. Since $\lim_{n \to +\infty} \ln(n+1) = +\infty$, by comparison, $\lim_{n \to +\infty} H_n = +\infty$.
3. $u_{n+1} - u_n = \frac{1}{n+1} - \ln(n+1) + \ln(n) = \frac{1}{n+1} - \int_n^{n+1} \frac{1}{t} dt \leq 0$ by the earlier inequality. The sequence is non-increasing and bounded below by 0, so it converges to a real number $\gamma \in [0, 1]$.

**Solution 9.**
1. $W_0 = \int_0^{\pi/2} 1 dt = \frac{\pi}{2}$. $W_1 = \int_0^{\pi/2} \sin t dt = [-\cos t]_0^{\pi/2} = 1$.
2. $W_n = \int_0^{\pi/2} \sin^{n-1}(t) \sin(t) dt$. By integration by parts with $u = \sin^{n-1}t$ and $v' = \sin t$:
   $W_n = [-\cos t \sin^{n-1}t]_0^{\pi/2} + (n-1)\int_0^{\pi/2} \cos^2(t)\sin^{n-2}(t)dt = 0 + (n-1)\int_0^{\pi/2} (1 - \sin^2 t)\sin^{n-2}t dt = (n-1)W_{n-2} - (n-1)W_n$.
   Hence $n W_n = (n-1) W_{n-2} \implies W_n = \frac{n-1}{n} W_{n-2}$.
3. On $[0, \pi/2]$, $0 \leq \sin t \leq 1 \implies \sin^{n+1} t \leq \sin^n t$. Hence $W_{n+1} \leq W_n$.
   Furthermore, $\frac{W_{n+1}}{W_n} \geq \frac{W_{n+2}}{W_n} = \frac{n+1}{n+2} \to 1$. By the squeeze theorem, $\lim \frac{W_{n+1}}{W_n} = 1$.
4. Unrolling $W_{2n}$ and $W_{2n+1}$ by cascading recurrence, their ratio yields Wallis's infinite product converging to $\pi$.

**Solution 10.**
By integration by parts: $u(x) = x \implies u'(x) = 1$, and $v'(x) = \cos(nx) \implies v(x) = \frac{\sin(nx)}{n}$.
$\int_0^\pi x \cos(nx) dx = \left[x \frac{\sin(nx)}{n}\right]_0^\pi - \int_0^\pi \frac{\sin(nx)}{n} dx = 0 - \left[-\frac{\cos(nx)}{n^2}\right]_0^\pi = \frac{\cos(n\pi) - \cos(0)}{n^2} = \frac{(-1)^n - 1}{n^2}$.
This integral equals $-\frac{2}{n^2}$ if $n$ is odd, and 0 if $n$ is even.

**Solution 11.**
$y' = a y - \frac{a}{K} y^2$. Dividing by $y^2$: $\frac{y'}{y^2} = \frac{a}{y} - \frac{a}{K}$.
Since $z = \frac{1}{y}$, $z' = -\frac{y'}{y^2}$.
The equation becomes $-z' = az - \frac{a}{K} \iff z' + az = \frac{a}{K}$.
This is a standard first-order linear differential equation!
Homogeneous solution: $z_H = C e^{-at}$. Particular solution: $z_P = \frac{1}{K}$.
Hence $z(t) = \frac{1}{K} + C e^{-at}$, which yields $y(t) = \frac{K}{1 + C K e^{-at}}$.
This is the universal logistic sigmoid growth curve.

**Solution 12.**
Let $N = 2^{p-1} M_p$ with $M_p = 2^p - 1$ prime.
Since $M_p$ is an odd prime, the divisors of $N$ are of the form $2^k$ (for $0 \leq k \leq p-1$) and $2^k M_p$ (for $0 \leq k \leq p-1$).
The sum of all divisors of $N$ is:
$\sigma(N) = \left(\sum_{k=0}^{p-1} 2^k\right) (1 + M_p) = (2^p - 1)(1 + 2^p - 1) = (2^p - 1) 2^p = 2 \times [2^{p-1}(2^p - 1)] = 2N$.
Since the sum of all divisors is $2N$, the sum of proper divisors is $2N - N = N$. The number is perfect.

**Solution 13.**
1. The random variable $X$ takes the value $2^k$ with probability $(1/2)^k$.
   $E(X) = \sum_{k=1}^{+\infty} 2^k \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^k = \sum_{k=1}^{+\infty} 1 = 1 + 1 + 1 + \dots = +\infty$.
2. The expected value is infinite, yet few people would pay even 1,000 euros to play, because the probability of winning more than 16 euros is only $\frac{1}{16} \approx 6\%$. This paradox led Daniel Bernoulli (1738) to introduce the concept of logarithmic **marginal utility** of wealth.

**Solution 14.**
Applying column operations $C_3 \leftarrow C_3 - a C_2$ and $C_2 \leftarrow C_2 - a C_1$, the first row becomes $(1 \ 0 \ 0)$, which allows immediately factoring out $(b-a)$ and $(c-a)$, leaving $(c-b)$.
If the three points have distinct $x$-coordinates, this determinant is nonzero, proving that the coefficient matrix for the polynomial $P(x) = \alpha x^2 + \beta x + \gamma$ is invertible: there exists a unique parabola passing through 3 non-vertically aligned points.

**Solution 15.**
If $P(z)$ has no root, $|P(z)| > 0$ everywhere. Because $|P(z)| \to +\infty$ as $|z| \to +\infty$, a global minimum $m > 0$ is attained at some point $z_0$. Expanding locally $P(z_0 + h) = P(z_0) + c h^k + \dots$ and selecting the argument of $h$ such that $c h^k$ directly opposes $P(z_0)$, one finds a nearby point where the modulus is strictly less than $m$, contradicting the minimality of $m$ (d'Alembert's lemma). Thus $m = 0$, and $P$ has a root.

**Solution 16.**
Expressing $\sin((2m+1)x)$ using de Moivre's formula and factoring by $\sin^{2m+1}(x)$, the roots of the polynomial are $\cot^2\left(\frac{k\pi}{2m+1}\right)$ for $k = 1, \dots, m$.
The sum of roots is given by Vieta's formulas: $\sum_{k=1}^m \cot^2\left(\frac{k\pi}{2m+1}\right) = \frac{m(2m-1)}{3}$.
Using the inequality $\cot^2 \theta < \frac{1}{\theta^2} < 1 + \cot^2 \theta$ and taking $m \to +\infty$, the squeeze theorem yields the exact value $\frac{\pi^2}{6}$.

**Solution 17.**
Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ b & c \end{pmatrix}$. Its characteristic discriminant is $\Delta = (a+c)^2 - 4(ac - b^2) = (a-c)^2 + 4b^2$.
Since $(a-c)^2 \geq 0$ and $4b^2 \geq 0$, $\Delta \geq 0$ always! The eigenvalues are always real. If $b \neq 0$, $\Delta > 0$ and the two eigenvalues are distinct, with mutually orthogonal eigenvectors ($\vec{v}_1 \cdot \vec{v}_2 = 0$).

**Solution 18.**
For any real $t$, $P(t) = \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i t + y_i)^2 \geq 0$ as a sum of squares.
Expanding: $P(t) = \left(\sum x_i^2\right) t^2 + 2\left(\sum x_i y_i\right) t + \left(\sum y_i^2\right) \geq 0$.
This quadratic polynomial in $t$ maintains a constant non-negative sign. Its reduced discriminant $\Delta'$ must therefore be non-positive:
$\Delta' = \left(\sum x_i y_i\right)^2 - \left(\sum x_i^2\right)\left(\sum y_i^2\right) \leq 0$, establishing the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. The continuous version for integrals follows as the limit of Riemann sums.

**Solution 19.**
By induction, $|u_{n+1} - u_n| \leq k^n |u_1 - u_0|$.
For $p \geq 1$, $|u_{n+p} - u_n| \leq \sum_{j=n}^{n+p-1} |u_{j+1} - u_j| \leq |u_1 - u_0| \sum_{j=n}^{+\infty} k^j = \frac{k^n}{1-k} |u_1 - u_0|$.
The sequence is Cauchy in a complete closed interval, so it converges to a unique fixed point $\alpha$. This theorem underpins numerical methods for solving differential and integral equations.

**Solution 20.**
By the Central Limit Theorem (CLT), if $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n$ are independent, identically distributed random variables with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, the standardized variable $\frac{\sum X_i - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}$ converges in distribution to the standard normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. The bell curve is the universal attractor of any macroscopic phenomenon resulting from a multitude of minute, independent microscopic variations.

**Solution 21.**
For the tetrahedron: $V=4, E=6, F=4 \implies 4 - 6 + 4 = 2$.
For the cube: $V=8, E=12, F=6 \implies 8 - 12 + 6 = 2$.
For the octahedron: $V=6, E=12, F=8 \implies 2$.
For the dodecahedron: $V=20, E=30, F=12 \implies 2$.
For the icosahedron: $V=12, E=30, F=20 \implies 2$.
This quantity $\chi = V - E + F$ is invariant under homeomorphic deformations of the surface: for a torus (doughnut), it equals 0. This marked the birth of algebraic topology under Henri Poincaré.

**Solution 22.**
For $u_0 = 7$: $7 \to 22 \to 11 \to 34 \to 17 \to 52 \to 26 \to 13 \to 40 \to 20 \to 10 \to 5 \to 16 \to 8 \to 4 \to 2 \to 1$ (loop $1 \to 4 \to 2 \to 1$).
