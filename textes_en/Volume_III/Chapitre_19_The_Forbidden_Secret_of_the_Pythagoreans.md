### 1. The Problem: The White Brotherhood of Croton

Twenty-five hundred years ago, in the sun-drenched maritime city of Croton in southern Italy, flourished a cloistered community that both fascinated and unsettled the ancient Mediterranean: **the Pythagorean school**.

Its initiates dressed in flowing white linen robes. They ate no meat, held no private property, and observed up to five years of absolute silence to master their unruly thoughts. They recognized one another through a secret emblem drawn in the palm of the hand: the five-pointed star pentagram.

Engraved upon the portal of their academy was their supreme doctrine:
$$\mathbf{\text{“ALL IS NUMBER”}}$$

To master **Pythagoras** and his disciples, this was no poetic figure of speech. They firmly believed that **the entire cosmos—the orbital dance of planets, the changing seasons, the justice of the laws, and the music of the lyre—was built strictly upon whole integers ($1, 2, 3, 4\dots$) and their rational fractions ($\frac{1}{2}, \frac{2}{3}, \frac{3}{4}\dots$).**

When you pinch a vibrating lyre string at its exact midpoint ($\frac{1}{2}$), it rings with the same pitch one octave higher. When you pinch it at two-thirds of its length ($\frac{2}{3}$), it sings a pure fifth.  
To them, all of creation was harmony, light, and integer proportion: every physical length across the earth could be measured by a ratio of whole numbers!

Until the fateful day when a young disciple named **Hippasus of Metapontum** took a wooden stylus and drew a seemingly innocent figure in the dust.  
He did not sketch an elaborate polygon. He drew the simplest, humblest square imaginable:  
**a square whose sides measure exactly 1 meter.**

Then, he connected two opposite corners with a straight line: he drew **the diagonal**.

```
                 1 meter
           ┌─────────────────┐
           │               / │
           │             /   │
           │           /     │
     1 m   │      d  /       │ 1 m
           │       /         │
           │     /           │
           │   /             │
           │ /               │
           └─────────────────┘
                 1 meter
```

Hippasus set out to calculate the exact numerical length $d$ of this diagonal. He applied the great theorem of his master. And what he uncovered was so terrifying to the brotherhood that they swore him to secrecy under penalty of death…

### 2. The Right Triangle and Its Treasure: The Hypotenuse

Before reliving the intellectual tragedy of Hippasus, let us explore the royal figure of geometry: the **right-angled triangle**.

```
                     B
                     | \
                     |   \
                     |     \
     Leg of the      |       \  Hypotenuse (the longest side)
     right angle     |         \
                     |           \
                     |____         \
                     A    |───────── C
                     Leg of the right angle
```

> **📐 Definition — Right Triangle and Hypotenuse**  
> - A **right-angled triangle** (or right triangle) is a triangle possessing one **right angle** ($90^\circ$).  
> - The side directly facing the right angle is called the **hypotenuse**.  
> - The hypotenuse is **always the strictly longest** of the three sides in any right triangle.  
> - The two shorter sides that meet to form the right angle are called the **legs** (or sides of the right angle).

> **💡 Where Does This Strange Word Come From?** The word *hypotenuse* originates from the ancient Greek *hupoteinousa*, formed from *hupo* (“under”) and *teinein* (“to stretch”). The hypotenuse is literally the string “stretched beneath” the right angle, just like the taut bowstring of an archer or the staysail stay of a ship.

### 3. The Pythagorean Theorem: The Dance of Three Squares

The theorem is not originally an abstract algebraic formula, but an astonishing truth about the **geometric areas of squares** resting against the triangle's flanks.

> **📐 The Pythagorean Theorem (Geometric Statement)**  
> In any right-angled triangle, the area of the square constructed upon the hypotenuse is equal to the sum of the areas of the two squares constructed upon the two legs.

```
                         ┌─────────┐
                         │         │ Area b²
                         │         │
                         └─────────┘
                              b
                           ┌─────┐
                           │   / │
                         a │ /   │ c
                           └─────┘
                         ┌─┐    ┌───────────────┐
                 Area a² │ │    │               │
                         └─┘    │               │ Area c²
                                │               │
                                └───────────────┘
                                   a² + b² = c²
```

Denoting $a$ and $b$ as the lengths of the two legs, and $c$ as the length of the hypotenuse:
- The area of the first leg square is $a^2$ ($a \times a$).
- The area of the second leg square is $b^2$ ($b \times b$).
- The area of the hypotenuse square is $c^2$ ($c \times c$).

The foundational equality is written:
$$\mathbf{a^2 + b^2 = c^2}$$

If the triangle is labeled $ABC$ with its right angle at vertex $A$, the hypotenuse is $[BC]$, and the formula is expressed via its vertices:
$$BC^2 = AB^2 + AC^2$$

#### The Three Historic Visual Demonstrations

Why does this equality hold true for **every single right triangle in the universe**?  
Here are three visual proofs you can understand without writing a single algebraic equation, relying purely on your own eyes.

#### Proof 1: The Café-Tile Floor Puzzle
This is the simplest demonstration in existence, applying to an isosceles right triangle (half of a square):

```
         Small Square 1     Small Square 2           Large Hypotenuse Square
             ┌───┐              ┌───┐                           /\
             │ / │              │ / │                          /  \
             └───┘              └───┘                         /____\
            2 triangles        2 triangles                  4 triangles united!
```

Each small square resting on a leg contains 2 half-square right triangles, totaling $2 + 2 = 4$ triangles.  
The tilted square resting upon the hypotenuse contains **precisely 4 identical half-square triangles**! The combined area is visibly identical.

#### Proof 2: The Master Square in Two Arrangements
Draw a large square of side length $(a + b)$. We can arrange 4 identical copies of our right triangle (with sides $a, b, c$) inside this boundary in two distinct ways:

```
         Arrangement 1                           Arrangement 2
     ┌───────────┬───────┐                   ┌───────┬───────────┐
     │     a     │   b   │                   │\      │          /│
     │  Square   ├───────┤                   │ \  c  │   c     / │
     │    a²     │       │                   │  \    │        /  │
     ├───────────┤Square │                   │   \┌──┴──┐    /   │
     │ 4 right triangles │                   │    │Square   /    │
     │ nestled in corners│                   │ c  │ c²  │  c     │
     │           │  b²   │                   │    │     │        │
     └───────────┴───────┘                   └────┴─────┴────────┘
     Remaining Area = a² + b²                Remaining Area = c²
```

In both identical enclosing squares:
- On the left, we remove the 4 triangles: the remaining uncovered area is $a^2 + b^2$.
- On the right, we remove the exact same 4 triangles: the remaining uncovered area is $c^2$.  
Because we subtracted the exact same total area from identical large squares, what remains must be strictly equal:
$$a^2 + b^2 = c^2$$

#### Proof 3: Bhāskara's “Behold!”
In 12th-century India, mathematician Bhāskara arranged 4 identical right triangles of sides $a, b, c$ into an outer square of side $c$, leaving an interior empty square of side $(b - a)$:
$$\text{Total Area} = c^2 = 4 \times \left(\frac{a \times b}{2}\right) + (b - a)^2 = 2ab + (b^2 - 2ab + a^2) = a^2 + b^2$$
Beneath his diagram, Bhāskara wrote not a single sentence of explanation, but a single command: *“Behold!”*

#### Proof 4: Oliver Byrne's Color Semiotic Proof (1847)
In 1847, the civil engineer and mathematician Oliver Byrne published an extraordinary edition of *Euclid's Elements* in London, banishing alphabetical lettering in favor of vibrant primary colors: brilliant blues, bold reds, and warm ambers. The squares constructed upon the legs decompose into colored triangles of matching area, rendering $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ self-evident at a single glance. It is this masterpiece of visual semiotics that inspired the chromatic charter of the present treatise.

### 4. The Square Root: Journeying Backward from Square to Side

When using the Pythagorean Theorem to find an unknown side length, we typically compute $c^2$ (the area of the square), and need to find $c$ (the side length).  
To do so, we introduce a fundamental mathematical tool: **the radical symbol for square roots**.

```
                Side c = ?  ────────────►  Area c² = 25
                               squared
                                
                Side c = 5  ◄────────────  Area c² = 25
                              square root √
```

> **📐 Definition — Square Root**  
> For any non-negative real number $x \ge 0$, the **square root** of $x$, denoted $\sqrt{x}$, is the unique **non-negative** number whose square equals $x$:
> $$(\sqrt{x})^2 = x$$
> - The symbol $\sqrt{\phantom{x}}$ is called the **radical sign**. It evolved from a handwritten letter *r*, the initial of the Latin word *radix* (“root”).  
> - $\sqrt{x}$ reads aloud as **“the square root of $x$”**.

### 5. The Converse and Contrapositive of Pythagoras

Just like Thales' Theorem, Pythagoras' Theorem functions in both logical directions:
1. **Direct Theorem:** Right triangle $\Longrightarrow a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ (used to **calculate unknown side lengths**).
2. **Converse Theorem:** If in a triangle the square of the longest side equals the sum of the squares of the two shorter sides ($c^2 = a^2 + b^2$), then **the triangle is a right-angled triangle** (with its right angle directly facing side $c$).
3. **Contrapositive:** If $c^2 \neq a^2 + b^2$, then **the triangle is not a right triangle**.

This converse explains the empirical trick of the Egyptian rope-stretchers with their thirteen-knot rope (introduced in Chapter 15):  
With side lengths $3$, $4$, and $5$:
$$3^2 + 4^2 = 9 + 16 = 25 \quad \text{and} \quad 5^2 = 25$$
Because $3^2 + 4^2 = 5^2$, the triangle is **guaranteed to form a right angle**!  
The integer triplet $(3, 4, 5)$ is called a **Pythagorean triple**. Other famous integer triples include $(5, 12, 13)$, $(8, 15, 17)$, and $(7, 24, 25)$.

### 6. The Pythagorean Crisis: The Scandal of $\sqrt{2}$

Let us return to Hippasus of Metapontum and his humble square measuring 1 meter on each side.  
Applying the Pythagorean Theorem to the diagonal of the unit square:
$$d^2 = 1^2 + 1^2 = 1 + 1 = 2$$
The diagonal length $d$ is therefore:
$$d = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.41421356\dots$$

Hippasus sought the exact integer fraction $\frac{p}{q}$ capable of producing $\sqrt{2}$:
- He tried $\frac{7}{5} = 1.4 \implies \left(\frac{7}{5}\right)^2 = \frac{49}{25} = 1.96$ (too small!).
- He tried $\frac{10}{7} \approx 1.428 \implies \left(\frac{10}{7}\right)^2 = \frac{100}{49} = 2.04$ (too large!).
- He tested $\frac{1414}{1000}$, $\frac{17}{12}$, $\frac{577}{408}$... every candidate fraction missed the mark by a whisker!

Then Hippasus experienced a flash of terrifying genius: he constructed a rigorous proof showing that **no such fraction exists, and no such fraction could EVER exist**.

#### The Historic Proof by Contradiction (Parity Proof)

This proof stands as one of the supreme peaks of human intellect. Follow the logic step-by-step:

> **✍️ Formal Proof — The Irrationality of $\sqrt{2}$**  
>  
> 1. **The Counter-Hypothesis (Assumption for Contradiction):**  
>    Suppose the opposite of our claim: assume there exists a fraction of integers $\frac{p}{q}$ equal to $\sqrt{2}$.  
>    Assume this fraction is written in **irreducible form** (simplified to lowest terms: $p$ and $q$ cannot both be even numbers, or we would have divided them by 2).  
>  
> 2. **Square Both Sides:**  
>    $$\sqrt{2} = \frac{p}{q} \implies 2 = \frac{p^2}{q^2} \implies p^2 = 2 \times q^2$$  
>  
> 3. **Deduce that $p$ Is an Even Number:**  
>    Since $p^2 = 2 \times q^2$, $p^2$ is a multiple of 2, so $p^2$ is **even**.  
>    The square of an odd number is always odd ($3^2 = 9, 5^2 = 25$). Therefore, if $p^2$ is even, the integer $p$ itself must be **even**!  
>    We can write $p = 2k$ for some integer $k$.  
>  
> 4. **Deduce that $q$ Is ALSO an Even Number:**  
>    Substitute $p = 2k$ into our equation:  
>    $$(2k)^2 = 2q^2 \implies 4k^2 = 2q^2$$  
>    Divide both sides by 2:  
>    $$2k^2 = q^2 \quad \text{or} \quad q^2 = 2k^2$$  
>    Now $q^2$ is also a multiple of 2, meaning $q^2$ is even, which forces $q$ to be **even**!  
>  
> 5. **The Blatant Contradiction:**  
>    Stop and consider what we have just established: $p$ is even AND $q$ is even!  
>    Both numbers are divisible by 2.  
>    Yet this flatly contradicts our initial premise that fraction $\frac{p}{q}$ was **irreducible**!  
>  
> 6. **Conclusion:**  
>    Our initial assumption must be false: **$\sqrt{2}$ can never be expressed as an integer fraction $\frac{p}{q}$.**

This discovery sent shockwaves through the ancient world. The Pythagoreans termed such lengths *aloga*—“the unspeakable” or “that which cannot be spoken aloud.”  
Today, we call them **irrational numbers** (from the Latin *ratio*, meaning ratio or proportion).
- A **rational number** ($\mathbb{Q}$) can be written as a fraction of two integers ($\frac{3}{4}, -\frac{5}{2}, \frac{1}{3} = 0.333\dots$). Its decimal digits terminate or eventually repeat in an endless cycle.
- An **irrational number** ($\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}, \pi\dots$) can never be expressed as a fraction. Its decimal digits unfold endlessly into eternity without ever repeating a fixed pattern!

> **🔍 Historical Legend — The Drowning of Hippasus**  
> According to ancient lore recounted by the philosopher Iamblichus, the orthodox Pythagoreans were so appalled by the collapse of their numerical theology that they threw Hippasus overboard during a sea voyage, ensuring that the waves devoured the man who had dared to uncover $\sqrt{2}$. Yet truth cannot be drowned: geometry had irrevocably crossed into a new epoch.

### 7. Beware of Pitfalls!

> **⚠️ Warning: Costly Errors!**  
> 1. **The Phantom Square Root of a Sum:**  
>    $$\sqrt{a + b} \neq \sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b} \quad !$$  
>    Verify with $a = 9$ and $b = 16$:  
>    - $\sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = \mathbf{5}$.  
>    - $\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16} = 3 + 4 = \mathbf{7}$.  
>    $5 \neq 7$! You must ALWAYS compute the addition underneath the radical before taking the square root.  
> 2. **Neglecting to Identify the Longest Side in the Converse:**  
>    When testing whether triangle $MNP$ is right-angled, square the longest side by itself, and sum the squares of the two shorter sides separately. Never set them equal beforehand!  
> 3. **Taking Square Roots of Negative Numbers:**  
>    $\sqrt{-9}$ is **undefined** within the real number system! Any real number multiplied by itself yields a non-negative result ($(+)\times(+) = +$ and $(-)\times(-) = +$).  
> 4. **Confusing Leg and Hypotenuse Calculations:**  
>    - To find the hypotenuse: **add** squares ($c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$).  
>    - To find a leg: **subtract** squares ($a = \sqrt{c^2 - b^2}$).

### 8. Solved Examples

#### Solved Example 1 — Calculating the Hypotenuse
Triangle $ABC$ is right-angled at $A$, with $AB = 6\text{ cm}$ and $AC = 8\text{ cm}$.  
Calculate the length of hypotenuse $[BC]$.

*Solution:*  
- Triangle $ABC$ is right-angled at $A$.  
- By the **Pythagorean Theorem**:  
  $$BC^2 = AB^2 + AC^2$$  
- Substitute known values:  
  $$BC^2 = 6^2 + 8^2 = 36 + 64 = 100$$  
- Because $BC$ is a geometric length, $BC > 0$:  
  $$BC = \sqrt{100} = 10\text{ cm}$$  
The hypotenuse measures exactly $10\text{ cm}$. ✔

#### Solved Example 2 — Finding a Leg: The Firefighter's Ladder
A $5\text{-meter}$ rescue ladder leans against a vertical wall.  
The foot of the ladder sits on the ground $1.40\text{ m}$ from the base of the wall.  
At what vertical height does the top of the ladder touch the wall?

```
             Vertical Wall
             | \
             |   \  Ladder (c = 5 m)
     h = ?   |     \
             |_______\ Ground
             | 1.40 m |
```

*Solution:*  
- The vertical wall and horizontal ground form a right angle. In right triangle $ABC$: hypotenuse $BC = 5\text{ m}$, base leg $AB = 1.40\text{ m}$, and vertical height $AC = h$.  
- By Pythagoras:  
  $$BC^2 = AB^2 + AC^2 \implies 5^2 = 1.4^2 + h^2$$  
- Isolate $h^2$:  
  $$h^2 = 5^2 - 1.4^2 = 25 - 1.96 = 23.04$$  
- Take the square root:  
  $$h = \sqrt{23.04} = 4.8\text{ meters}$$  
The ladder reaches a vertical height of $4.80\text{ meters}$. ✔

#### Solved Example 3 — Proving a Triangle Is Right-Angled
A mason drives three wooden stakes into the ground at distances $MN = 13\text{ m}$, $NP = 12\text{ m}$, and $MP = 5\text{ m}$.  
Will the stone foundation wall built on these stakes have a perfect square corner?

*Solution:*  
- The longest side is $[MN]$ with $MN = 13\text{ m}$.  
- Compute separately:  
  $$MN^2 = 13^2 = 169$$  
  $$MP^2 + NP^2 = 5^2 + 12^2 = 25 + 144 = 169$$  
- We observe:  
  $$MN^2 = MP^2 + NP^2$$  
- Conclusion:  
  By the **Converse of the Pythagorean Theorem**, triangle $MNP$ is right-angled at vertex $P$.  
The mason is guaranteed a true $90^\circ$ right angle at corner $P$. ✔

#### Solved Example 4 — Proving a Triangle Is NOT Right-Angled
Triangle $DEF$ has sides $DE = 7\text{ cm}$, $EF = 9\text{ cm}$, and $DF = 11\text{ cm}$.  
Is it a right triangle?

*Solution:*  
- Longest side is $[DF] = 11\text{ cm}$.  
- Compute separately:  
  $$DF^2 = 11^2 = 121$$  
  $$DE^2 + EF^2 = 7^2 + 9^2 = 49 + 81 = 130$$  
- We observe:  
  $$DF^2 \neq DE^2 + EF^2 \quad (121 \neq 130)$$  
- Conclusion:  
  By the **contrapositive of the Pythagorean Theorem**, triangle $DEF$ is not a right triangle. ✔

#### Solved Example 5 — Smartphone Screen Diagonal
A smartphone screen measures $7\text{ cm}$ in width and $15\text{ cm}$ in height.  
What is the length of its diagonal? Round to the nearest millimeter.

*Solution:*  
- The screen is rectangular; the diagonal forms a right triangle with width and height.  
- By Pythagoras:  
  $$d^2 = 7^2 + 15^2 = 49 + 225 = 274$$  
- Therefore:  
  $$d = \sqrt{274} \approx 16.5529\dots\text{ cm} \approx 16.6\text{ cm}$$  
The diagonal measures approximately $16.6\text{ cm}$ (about $6.5\text{ inches}$). ✔

### What You Must Remember

1. **Right Triangle & Hypotenuse:**  
   The hypotenuse faces the $90^\circ$ angle and is always the longest side.
2. **Pythagorean Theorem (Length Calculations):**  
   In right triangle $ABC$ with right angle at $A$:  
   $$BC^2 = AB^2 + AC^2$$  
   - Hypotenuse: $BC = \sqrt{AB^2 + AC^2}$.  
   - Leg: $AB = \sqrt{BC^2 - AC^2}$.
3. **Converse & Contrapositive (Right-Angle Test):**  
   - If $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$ ($c$ longest side) $\implies$ the triangle **is right-angled**.  
   - If $c^2 \neq a^2 + b^2 \implies$ the triangle **is not right-angled**.
4. **Square Roots & Irrationals:**  
   - $\sqrt{x}$ is the unique non-negative number whose square is $x$.  
   - $\sqrt{2}$ cannot be expressed as an integer fraction $\frac{p}{q}$: it is an **irrational number**.

**Exercise 11.**

**Exercise 12.**  
1. $h_1 = \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}$.  
2. $h_2 = \sqrt{(\sqrt{2})^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2 + 1} = \sqrt{3}$.  
3. Successive hypotenuses measure $\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}, \sqrt{4} = 2, \sqrt{5}, \dots, \sqrt{n}$.  
4. This is the **Spiral of Theodorus of Cyrene** (or Pythagorean Snail).

**Exercise 13.**  
1. Face diagonal: $d_1 = \sqrt{4^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{32} = 4\sqrt{2}\text{ cm}$.  
2. Space diagonal: $D = \sqrt{d_1^2 + c^2} = \sqrt{32 + 16} = \sqrt{48} = 4\sqrt{3}\text{ cm} \approx 6.93\text{ cm}$.  
3. General formula: $D = \sqrt{c^2 + c^2 + c^2} = c\sqrt{3}$.

**Exercise 14.**  
Unfold the box net. The shortest surface path connects the spider and fly along a straight hypotenuse on the unfolded cardboard:  
Along the length across the top lid: horizontal span $= 30 + 1 + 1 = 32\text{ cm}$, vertical span $= 12 + 12 = 24\text{ cm}$.  
$D = \sqrt{32^2 + 24^2} = \sqrt{1,024 + 576} = \sqrt{1,600} = 40\text{ cm}$.  
The shortest path is exactly $40\text{ cm}$!

**Exercise 15.**  
Assume $\sqrt{3} = \frac{p}{q}$ in lowest terms.  
Then $3 = \frac{p^2}{q^2} \implies p^2 = 3q^2$.  
So $p^2$ is a multiple of 3, implying $p$ is a multiple of 3 ($p = 3k$).  
Then $(3k)^2 = 3q^2 \implies 9k^2 = 3q^2 \implies q^2 = 3k^2$.  
So $q^2$ is a multiple of 3, meaning $q$ is also a multiple of 3.  
Both $p$ and $q$ share 3 as a common divisor, contradicting that $\frac{p}{q}$ was irreducible.  
Thus $\sqrt{3}$ is irrational.

### The Question That Opens What Follows

The Pythagorean Theorem allows us to calculate the diagonal straight-line distance between two points. But what happens when our points are not drawn upon a free blank sheet of paper, but scattered across a navigator's nautical chart, a pirate's treasure map, or the pixel grid of a computer screen?

How can we pinpoint any location on Earth with absolute accuracy using two signed numbers, positive or negative? And why does “minus times minus equal plus”? That is the secret of **René Descartes and the two trails of Treasure Island**!
