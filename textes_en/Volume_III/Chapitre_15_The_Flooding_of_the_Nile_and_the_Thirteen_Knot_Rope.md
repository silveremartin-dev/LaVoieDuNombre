### 1. The Problem: The Mud of the Nile and the Wrath of the Farmers

You are now ten or eleven years old. Let us leave our small village behind and journey to the banks of a mighty river: the **Nile**, in Egypt, more than three thousand years ago.

Every year, in the heart of summer, an extraordinary event occurs. Far to the south, in the highlands of Ethiopia, torrential rains pour down. The waters of the Nile swell, burst from their banks, and submerge the entire valley. For weeks on end, the dry earth vanishes beneath a vast sea of brown water.

For the Egyptians, this **annual flood** (*akhet*) is a supreme blessing: the receding river leaves behind a layer of intensely fertile black mud, called **silt**. Thanks to this mineral-rich mud, wheat and barley flourish in abundance. The ancient Egyptians affectionately named their homeland *Kemet*, “the Black Land.”

Yet when the waters recede in autumn, a nightmare begins for the villagers: **every single stone marker, every furrow and boundary peg separating the farm plots has been swept away, displaced, or buried beneath feet of thick mud.** The immense plain has become a uniform grey expanse devoid of the slightest landmark.

*“My boundary reached all the way to the tall date palm!”* shouts Ameni.  
*“Liar! It ended ten cubits further south!”* bellows Nakht, raising his hoe in fury.

To prevent bloody land disputes and restore order, the Pharaoh dispatches his most elite civil servants: the **royal surveyors**. Centuries later, Greek travelers would marvel at them and call them the **harpedonaptai**, which literally translates to “the rope-stretchers.”

Their mission is formidable: they must lay out **rectangular** fields once more. And to construct a rectangle, one must produce **right angles**. In your notebook, you simply reach for your plastic set square. But across a muddy field two hundred meters wide, a small hand square is completely useless: if your angle is off by just a single fraction of a degree at the start, two hundred meters down the field your line will have drifted by more than three meters… cutting directly into your neighbor's property!

How can one produce a flawless right angle directly upon the open ground, without a metal drafting square, without a telescope, and without a protractor? Before answering this question, we must understand what an angle truly is.

> **💡 Did You Know?** The word **geometry** originates from the ancient Greek *gê* (“the earth”) and *metron* (“measure”). Geometry was born, quite literally, as “the measurement of the earth.” The Greek historian Herodotus recorded that geometry was invented in Egypt out of the urgent practical necessity of re-measuring agricultural parcels after each Nile inundation.

### 2. What Is an Angle?

#### 2.1 The Intuition with Your Own Body

Extend your right arm straight out in front of you, perfectly horizontal. Keep your shoulder pinned in place and slowly pivot your arm to your right, like the hand of a clock sweeping across a dial.

- At first, your arm has barely moved: the **opening** between your starting direction and your current direction is narrow.
- When your arm points straight out to your right side, it has made a **quarter-turn**: this is a right angle.
- If you keep pivoting until your arm points straight behind you, you have completed a **half-turn**.
- And if you pivot all the way back to the front, you have made a **full turn**.

This is the essence of an angle: **the amount of rotation between two directions that originate from the same common point**. Your shoulder acts as the **vertex**, and the two pointing directions are its two **sides** (rays).

#### 2.2 Words, Notations, and Geometrical Symbols

Before proceeding further, let us recall the foundational notation of geometry:

| Written Form | Pronunciation | Exact Mathematical Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| $[AB]$ | “segment $A B$” | The straight line segment connecting $A$ and $B$, including both endpoints |
| $(AB)$ | “line $A B$” | The infinite straight line passing through $A$ and $B$, extending without end in both directions |
| $[AB)$ | “ray $A B$” | The ray starting at origin $A$, passing through $B$, and extending infinitely beyond $B$ |
| $AB$ | “$A B$” (no brackets) | The **length** of segment $[AB]$, which is a number (e.g., $5\text{ cm}$) |

> **📐 Definition — Angle**  
> An **angle** is the geometric figure formed by two rays sharing the same starting point. This shared starting point is the **vertex** of the angle; the two rays are its **sides**.

```
                 C
                /
               /      Side [BC)
              /
             /  ) ← The opening: this rotation is the angle
            B ─────────────────── A
          Vertex       Side [BA)
```

To denote this angle symbolically, we write:

$$\widehat{ABC} \quad \text{or} \quad \angle ABC$$

We read this aloud as **“angle $A B C$”**. The circumflex hat (or the $\angle$ sign) placed above the letters serves as a clear warning: *“I am not speaking of individual points, but of the rotational opening between these two rays.”*

> **⚠️ Warning: Common Pitfall!** In the notation $\widehat{ABC}$, **the middle letter must always designate the vertex**. $\widehat{ABC}$ and $\widehat{CBA}$ describe the exact same angle (vertex $B$), but $\widehat{BAC}$ is an entirely different angle whose vertex is $A$! When there is no risk of confusion, mathematicians often write simply $\widehat{B}$ (“angle $B$”).

#### 2.3 An Angle Is NOT a Length

Here lies the most widespread misunderstanding among apprentice mathematicians. Examine these two angles:

```
   Angle 1:                            Angle 2:
                                               /
        /                                     /
       /                                     /
      /                                     /
     /__                                   /
                                          /
                                         /______________________
```

Which of the two angles is larger? Many beginners reflexively answer “the second one,” simply because its printed rays are drawn much longer on the paper.  
**That is completely false!**  
The sides of an angle are rays: they extend infinitely into space, and we merely sketch a small portion of them. What truly counts is the **rotational opening**, never the length of the pencil strokes. The two angles shown above possess the exact same angular opening.

> **🖐️ Try It Yourself —** Take two pencils and bind them firmly together at one end with a tight rubber band, like a pair of compasses. Open them slightly. Now replace them with two long meter sticks, keeping the exact same opening: the angle has not changed by a single hair, only the length of the arms has changed!

### 3. Measuring an Angle: The Degree and the Protractor

#### 3.1 Why 360 Degrees?

To quantify rotation precisely, we require a standard **unit of measurement**. Approximately four thousand years ago, the astronomers and mathematicians of Babylon divided a complete circular turn into **360 equal slices**. Each elementary slice is called a **degree**, symbolized by a small raised circle:

$$1^\circ \quad \text{reads “one degree”}$$

Why did the ancients choose 360 instead of a round decimal number like 100? For several compelling reasons:
- The Babylonians computed in **sexagesimal (base 60)**; our 60 minutes in an hour and 60 seconds in a minute stem directly from their system, and $360 = 6 \times 60$.
- The solar year lasts roughly 365 days; to the ancient sky-watchers, the Sun appeared to advance across the zodiac by approximately one degree each day.
- Above all, **360 is exceptionally divisible**. It can be cleanly split into equal integer parts by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, 15, 18, 20, 24, 30, 36, 40, 45, 60, 72, 90, 120, and 180. A third of a turn, an eighth of a turn, a fifth of a turn—all yield whole integer degrees!

| Rotation | Fractional Turn | Exact Degree Measure |
|---|---|---|
| Complete turn | $1$ full turn | $360^\circ$ |
| Half-turn | $\frac{1}{2}$ turn | $180^\circ$ |
| Quarter-turn | $\frac{1}{4}$ turn | $90^\circ$ |
| Eighth-turn | $\frac{1}{8}$ turn | $45^\circ$ |
| Twelfth-turn (one hour on a clock face) | $\frac{1}{12}$ turn | $30^\circ$ |

#### 3.2 Families of Angles

> **📐 Definition — Angle Classification**  
> According to its measure $\theta$, an angle is classified as:
> - **Zero angle:** $\theta = 0^\circ$ (the two rays completely overlap).
> - **Acute angle:** $0^\circ < \theta < 90^\circ$ (sharp, pointed, narrower than a quarter-turn).
> - **Right angle:** $\theta = 90^\circ$ (the perpendicular corner).
> - **Obtuse angle:** $90^\circ < \theta < 180^\circ$ (wide, blunt, opened wider than a quarter-turn).
> - **Straight angle:** $\theta = 180^\circ$ (the two rays form a continuous straight line).
> - **Reflex angle:** $180^\circ < \theta < 360^\circ$ (an angle opening beyond a straight line).
> - **Full (perigon) angle:** $\theta = 360^\circ$ (one complete rotation).

```
    Acute            Right             Obtuse               Straight
      /                |                     /                 |                     /__              __|__ (□)         ___\_____       _____________._____________
```

In geometric drawings, a right angle is always highlighted by a **small square** nestled in the corner: $\llcorner$.

*Mnemonic tip:* **Acute** shares its Latin root with *needle* (sharp, piercing, narrow); **obtuse** shares its root with *blunt* (dull, wide open).

#### 3.3 Mastering the Protractor

A standard protractor is a transparent semicircular tool graduated from $0^\circ$ to $180^\circ$. It features **two concentric scales**: one reads from left to right, and the other reads from right to left. This dual scale is the source of nearly all beginner measurement blunders!

> **🧭 Method — Measuring an Angle $\widehat{ABC}$ with a Protractor**  
> 1. Place the **center point** of the protractor (the crosshair or small hole) directly over the **vertex** $B$.
> 2. Align the **baseline zero-mark** of the protractor perfectly along one of the rays, say $[BA)$.
> 3. Locate the scale **that starts at $0^\circ$ on that chosen ray**, follow it around the rim, and read the number where the second ray $[BC)$ crosses the curved graduation.
> 4. **Perform a visual sanity check:** Look at the angle with your naked eye! Is it acute or obtuse? Your numerical reading must agree with physical reality.

> **⚠️ Warning: Double Scale Trap!** If an angle is visibly acute but your reading says “$130^\circ$”, you have read from the wrong concentric ring! The correct reading is $180^\circ - 130^\circ = 50^\circ$. Asking yourself *“Is it sharp or blunt?”* prevents 99% of mistakes.

> **🧭 Method — Constructing an Angle of Given Measure (e.g., $\widehat{xOy} = 65^\circ$)**  
> 1. Draw a straight ray $[Ox)$ with a ruler.
> 2. Place the protractor center on point $O$, aligning the zero-line with $[Ox)$.
> 3. Starting from $0^\circ$ along $[Ox)$, count up to $65^\circ$ and make a crisp pencil tick on the paper.
> 4. Remove the protractor and use your ruler to draw ray $[Oy)$ from vertex $O$ through your tick mark.
> 5. Mark the angle with a small arc and label it $65^\circ$.

#### Solved Examples

**Solved Example 1 — Classifying Angles.**  
Classify each of the following angular measures: $17^\circ$; $90^\circ$; $91^\circ$; $179^\circ$; $180^\circ$; $45^\circ$.

*Solution:*  
$17^\circ$ and $45^\circ$ are **acute** (strictly between $0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$).  
$90^\circ$ is a **right** angle.  
$91^\circ$ and $179^\circ$ are **obtuse** (strictly between $90^\circ$ and $180^\circ$).  
$180^\circ$ is a **straight** angle.  
*Remark:* Even though $91^\circ$ looks almost identical to a right angle to the untrained eye, in geometry a single degree makes all the difference: it is strictly obtuse!

**Solved Example 2 — Clock Hands.**  
What angle is formed by the hands of an analog clock at 2:00? At 5:00?

*Solution:*  
A clock face represents a complete turn of $360^\circ$ partitioned into 12 hourly increments. Between any two consecutive numbers, the angle is:
$$360^\circ \div 12 = 30^\circ$$
At 2:00, the minute hand points at 12 and the hour hand at 2. There are 2 hourly intervals:
$$2 \times 30^\circ = 60^\circ \quad \text{(acute angle)}$$
At 5:00, there are 5 intervals:
$$5 \times 30^\circ = 150^\circ \quad \text{(obtuse angle)}$$

**Solved Example 3 — Reading a Protractor Without Confusion.**  
Leah places her protractor over angle $\widehat{DEF}$ (vertex $E$, with ray $[ED)$ aligned with the zero mark on the right). Ray $[EF)$ passes through the marks $40$ and $140$. The angle is clearly wide open. What is its measure?

*Solution:*  
Ray $[ED)$ lies on the **right** zero; we must follow the scale starting at $0$ on the right. Alternatively, using our visual check: the angle is visibly obtuse (wider than $90^\circ$), so it cannot possibly be $40^\circ$. Therefore, $\widehat{DEF} = 140^\circ$.

### 4. Perpendicular and Parallel Lines

#### 4.1 Two Intersecting Lines

When two straight lines cross at a point, they form **four angles**:

```
                \     
            ①    \    ②
         ─────────●─────────
            ④      \  ③
                    ```

- Angles ① and ③ are called **vertically opposite angles** (or vertical angles): they sit face-to-face across the intersection, like the two blades of an open pair of scissors. They have **identical measures**.
- Angles ① and ② lie side-by-side along a straight line: their measures add up to $180^\circ$. They are called **supplementary angles**.

> **📐 Vocabulary — Angle Pairs**  
> - Two angles are **supplementary** if their sum equals $180^\circ$.
> - Two angles are **complementary** if their sum equals $90^\circ$.
> - Two angles are **adjacent** if they share the same vertex, have a common side, and lie on opposite sides of that common side.

**Why are vertically opposite angles strictly equal?**  
Because ① + ② $= 180^\circ$ and ② + ③ $= 180^\circ$. Both ① and ③ complete the exact same angle ② to reach a straight line ($180^\circ$). Therefore:
$$\text{Angle ①} = 180^\circ - \text{Angle ②} = \text{Angle ③}$$
This is our very first formal **geometric proof**: we did not rely on physical measurements, but solely on deductive logical reasoning!

#### 4.2 Perpendicular Lines

If just one of the four angles at an intersection is a right angle ($90^\circ$), then all four angles are automatically right angles ($180^\circ - 90^\circ = 90^\circ$).

> **📐 Definition — Perpendicular Lines**  
> Two lines are **perpendicular** if they intersect at a right angle ($90^\circ$). We write:
> $$(d_1) \perp (d_2)$$
> and read “line $d_1$ is perpendicular to line $d_2$.” The symbol $\perp$ is itself a graphic depiction of two perpendicular lines!

#### 4.3 Parallel Lines

> **📐 Definition — Parallel Lines**  
> Two lines lying in the same plane are **parallel** if they never intersect—no matter how far they are extended—or if they are identical (coincident). We write:
> $$(d_1) \parallel (d_2) \quad \text{or} \quad (d_1) \,//\, (d_2)$$
> and read “$d_1$ is parallel to $d_2$.” The symbol depicts two side-by-side lines that never meet, like straight railway tracks.

Three fundamental properties govern these relationships in a Euclidean plane:

> **📐 Fundamental Euclidean Properties —**  
> - **P1.** If two lines are perpendicular to the same third line, then they are parallel to each other.  
> - **P2.** If two lines are parallel, any third line perpendicular to one is also perpendicular to the other.  
> - **P3.** If two lines are parallel to the same third line, they are parallel to each other.

```
   P1 :       |         |               P2 :     ════════════  (d1)
              |         |                              |
       ───────┼─────────┼───────                 ══════╪═════  (d2)
              |         |                              |
             (d1)      (d2)                           (d3) ⊥ (d1) ⟹ ⊥ (d2)
     (d1) ⊥ (d) and (d2) ⊥ (d) ⟹ (d1) // (d2)
```

The logical symbol $\Longrightarrow$ reads “therefore” or “implies”: the truth of the statement on the left guarantees the truth of the statement on the right.

#### 4.4 Construction with Tools

> **🧭 Method — Drawing the Perpendicular to $(d)$ Through Point $A$ (Set Square)**  
> Place one side of the set square's right angle firmly along line $(d)$. Slide the set square along $(d)$ until its other perpendicular side meets point $A$. Draw the line through $A$.

> **🧭 Method — Drawing the Parallel to $(d)$ Through Point $A$ (Ruler + Set Square)**  
> First, draw the line $(p)$ perpendicular to $(d)$ passing through $A$. Then construct the line perpendicular to $(p)$ passing through $A$. By property **P1**, this second line is strictly parallel to $(d)$.

> **🧭 Method — The Compass-Only Perpendicular (The Master Builder's Method)**  
> 1. Place the compass point at $A$ and strike an arc that cuts line $(d)$ at two points, $M$ and $N$.
> 2. With a wider opening, place the compass point at $M$, then at $N$, and strike two intersecting arcs at a point $P$ (on the other side of $(d)$).
> 3. Draw line $(AP)$: it is guaranteed to be perpendicular to $(d)$.  
> *Why?* Both $A$ and $P$ are equidistant from $M$ and $N$, placing them on the **perpendicular bisector** of segment $[MN]$.

This compass method is precisely what Egyptian and medieval master builders performed on vast building sites using **two pegs and a piece of cord** functioning as a giant compass!

#### 4.5 Distance from a Point to a Line

Imagine you are standing at point $A$ in the middle of a field and wish to reach a straight paved road $(d)$ as quickly as possible. Instinctively, you walk straight towards the road at a **perpendicular angle**.

> **📐 Definition — Distance from a Point to a Line**  
> The **distance from point $A$ to line $(d)$** is the length of segment $[AH]$, where $H$ is the foot of the perpendicular dropped from $A$ onto $(d)$. It represents the shortest possible path connecting $A$ to $(d)$.

#### Solved Example 4 — Angles Around an Intersection

Two straight lines intersect at $O$. One of the angles measures $38^\circ$. Find the measures of the remaining three angles.

*Solution:*  
The vertically opposite angle also measures $38^\circ$. The two adjacent angles are supplementary to $38^\circ$:
$$180^\circ - 38^\circ = 142^\circ$$
The four angles measure $38^\circ$, $142^\circ$, $38^\circ$, and $142^\circ$.  
*Verification:* $38^\circ + 142^\circ + 38^\circ + 142^\circ = 360^\circ$ (one full rotation). ✔

### 5. The Sum of the Angles in Any Triangle: Always $180^\circ$

Here stands one of the most stunning and universal truths of all geometry: take any triangle in the universe—whether microscopic or spanning light-years, razor-sharp or flat and obtuse—and measure its three internal angles. Add them together. **The sum will always be exactly $180^\circ$!**

$$\widehat{A} + \widehat{B} + \widehat{C} = 180^\circ$$

Let us explore three distinct ways to convince ourselves of this eternal truth.

#### 5.1 Proof 1: The Paper-Tear Experiment (Physical Intuition)

1. Take a sheet of blank paper and draw any triangle you like with a ruler.
2. Color each of the three corners with a different colored pencil: Blue at vertex $A$, Red at vertex $B$, and Amber at vertex $C$.
3. Carefully cut out the triangle, then tear off the three colored corners.
4. Arrange the three points together on a flat tabletop so their tips meet at a single common point, placing the colored edges snugly side-by-side:

```
        Paper Triangle                        Torn Corners Reassembled
              A (Blue)                                      A
             /\                                            /            /  \                                  Blue    /  \   Red
           /    \                                        /      (Red) B /______\ C (Amber)              ──────────────●──────●──────────────
                                              Amber           Line = 180°
```

The three angles fit together seamlessly to form a perfect straight edge! Since a straight edge represents a straight angle of $180^\circ$, their sum is demonstrably $180^\circ$.

#### 5.2 Proof 2: The Euclidean Proof (Deductive Rigor)

To transform this sensory experiment into a watertight mathematical proof, we follow Euclid's luminous reasoning using parallel lines:

```
                  D ────────── A ────────── E       Line (DE) // (BC)
                              /                     Angle B' /   \ Angle C'
                            /                                /                                 /                                  B───────────C
```

1. Given any triangle $ABC$, construct line $(DE)$ passing through vertex $A$ parallel to the opposite base $(BC)$.
2. The three angles meeting at point $A$ along the straight line $(DE)$ form a straight angle:
   $$\widehat{DAB} + \widehat{BAC} + \widehat{CAE} = 180^\circ$$
3. Because $(DE)$ is parallel to $(BC)$, line $(AB)$ acts as a transversal line cutting two parallel lines:
   - Angle $\widehat{DAB}$ and angle $\widehat{ABC}$ are **alternate interior angles**, so they are equal: $\widehat{DAB} = \widehat{B}$.
   - Similarly, angle $\widehat{CAE}$ and angle $\widehat{ACB}$ are alternate interior angles, so: $\widehat{CAE} = \widehat{C}$.
4. Substituting these equalities into our sum yields:
   $$\widehat{B} + \widehat{A} + \widehat{C} = 180^\circ$$
The proof is complete!

#### 5.3 Proof 3: The Walker's Turn (Algorithmic / Robotic View)

Imagine a tiny explorer (or a robot) walking along the perimeter of triangle $ABC$:
- The walker starts at $A$, walks toward $B$, and at vertex $B$ turns left by exterior angle $\theta_1 = 180^\circ - \widehat{B}$.
- She walks to $C$ and turns left by $\theta_2 = 180^\circ - \widehat{C}$.
- She walks back to $A$ and turns left to face her initial heading: $\theta_3 = 180^\circ - \widehat{A}$.

Upon returning to $A$, the walker has made exactly **one full rotation** ($360^\circ$) in the plane:
$$(180^\circ - \widehat{A}) + (180^\circ - \widehat{B}) + (180^\circ - \widehat{C}) = 360^\circ$$
$$540^\circ - (\widehat{A} + \widehat{B} + \widehat{C}) = 360^\circ$$
$$\widehat{A} + \widehat{B} + \widehat{C} = 540^\circ - 360^\circ = 180^\circ$$

### 6. Special Triangles and the Secret of the Thirteen-Knot Rope

#### 6.1 The Equilateral Triangle
An **equilateral triangle** has three sides of equal length. By symmetry, its three internal angles are also strictly equal:
$$\widehat{A} = \widehat{B} = \widehat{C} = \frac{180^\circ}{3} = 60^\circ$$
Every equilateral triangle in the universe has three $60^\circ$ angles.

#### 6.2 The Isosceles Triangle
An **isosceles triangle** has two sides of equal length. The angles sitting opposite those equal sides (called the **base angles**) are strictly equal:
- If the apex angle is $40^\circ$, the two base angles share the remaining $140^\circ$ equally:
  $$\frac{180^\circ - 40^\circ}{2} = 70^\circ \quad \text{each}$$

#### 6.3 The Right-Angled Triangle
A **right-angled triangle** has one right angle ($90^\circ$). The other two acute angles must therefore absorb the remaining $90^\circ$:
$$\widehat{A} + \widehat{B} = 90^\circ$$
The two acute angles of a right triangle are always **complementary**. If one measures $35^\circ$, the other must measure $90^\circ - 35^\circ = 55^\circ$.

#### 6.4 The Thirteen-Knot Rope: The Harpedonaptai's Secret Weapon

We can now resolve the mystery posed in our opening problem: how did the Egyptian rope-stretchers lay out a flawless right angle on the ground?

They used a single length of rope tied with **thirteen knots spaced at identical intervals**, and joined its ends to form a continuous closed loop:
```
  Knot 1 ── Knot 2 ── Knot 3 ── Knot 4 ── ... ── Knot 13 (tied to Knot 1)
  │◄──────── 12 equal intervals between the 13 knots ────────►│
```

Because knot 13 joins knot 1, the loop contains exactly **12 equal segments**.
The surveyors drove three wooden stakes into the ground and pulled the rope tight around them, distributing the segments as follows:
- **3 segments** along the first side (from knot 1 to knot 4);
- **4 segments** along the second side (from knot 4 to knot 8);
- **5 segments** along the third side (from knot 8 back to knot 13/1).

$$3 + 4 + 5 = 12 \text{ segments}$$

```
                          Knot 4 (Stake)
                             ▲
                             |                     3 units  |                                |     \  5 units
                             |                                    |____                                  ■────┴─────► Knot 8 (Stake)
                       Knot 1 & 13       4 units
                         (Stake)
```

When pulled taut, the angle formed between the side of 3 units and the side of 4 units is **guaranteed to be an absolute, mathematical right angle ($90^\circ$)**!  
*Why does this work?* Because $3^2 + 4^2 = 9 + 16 = 25 = 5^2$. This is the famous **$(3, 4, 5)$ Pythagorean triple**, an algebraic harmony discovered empirically by Egyptian builders more than a millennium before Pythagoras was born!

### 7. 🛠️ The Boundary Case Laboratory

To sharpen our mathematical thinking, let us test the boundaries of our rules where everyday intuition falters:

#### Case 1: Can a Triangle Have Two Right Angles?
Suppose a triangle had two right angles: $\widehat{A} = 90^\circ$ and $\widehat{B} = 90^\circ$.  
The sum of all three angles must equal $180^\circ$:
$$\widehat{C} = 180^\circ - (90^\circ + 90^\circ) = 0^\circ$$
An angle of $0^\circ$ means the two sides are parallel and never meet! In a Euclidean flat plane, **it is strictly impossible for a triangle to possess two right angles**.

> **🌍 Beyond Flat Space — Spherical Geometry:**  
> However, on the curved surface of a sphere—such as planet Earth—lines of longitude meet the equator at $90^\circ$ right angles, and they also meet at the North Pole! A triangle drawn from the North Pole down to the equator at $0^\circ$ longitude, along the equator to $90^\circ$ East longitude, and back up to the pole has **three right angles** ($90^\circ + 90^\circ + 90^\circ = 270^\circ$). The $180^\circ$ theorem is true only in **flat Euclidean space**!

#### Case 2: The Degenerate (Flat) Triangle
What happens if the vertex $C$ is pushed down onto the base segment $[AB]$?  
The angle $\widehat{C}$ opens all the way to $180^\circ$, while $\widehat{A}$ and $\widehat{B}$ collapse to $0^\circ$:
$$0^\circ + 180^\circ + 0^\circ = 180^\circ$$
The triangle flattens into a single straight line segment. It is called a **degenerate triangle**.

#### Case 3: Angles in Non-Coplanar Space
In three-dimensional space, two lines that never intersect are not necessarily parallel! Think of a bridge crossing high above a highway: the road below and the bridge above never meet, yet they point in different directions. Such lines are called **skew lines** (or non-coplanar lines). Parallelism requires the lines to lie within the **same flat plane**.

### What You Must Remember

1. **Nature of an Angle:** An angle measures an opening of rotation between two rays sharing a common vertex. It does not depend on the length of the drawn strokes.
2. **Standard Units:** A full turn is $360^\circ$; a straight angle is $180^\circ$; a right angle is $90^\circ$.
3. **Protractor Sanity Check:** Always verify whether the angle is acute ($< 90^\circ$) or obtuse ($> 90^\circ$) before recording your measurement.
4. **Opposite and Supplementary Angles:** Vertically opposite angles are equal. Adjacent angles along a straight line are supplementary (sum $= 180^\circ$).
5. **Sum of Angles in a Triangle:** For any flat triangle, $\widehat{A} + \widehat{B} + \widehat{C} = 180^\circ$.
6. **Special Triangles:**
   - Equilateral: three equal sides, three $60^\circ$ angles.
   - Isosceles: two equal sides, two equal base angles.
   - Right: one $90^\circ$ angle, acute angles sum to $90^\circ$.
7. **The Thirteen-Knot Rope:** A closed loop with 12 equal intervals formed into a $3-4-5$ triangle guarantees a perfect $90^\circ$ corner.

**Exercise 1.**  
$12^\circ$ and $89^\circ$: acute.

**Exercise 2.**  
The sum of angles in triangle $ABC$ is $180^\circ$:  
$\widehat{C} = 180^\circ - (52^\circ + 68^\circ) = 180^\circ - 120^\circ = 60^\circ$.

**Exercise 3.**  
The acute angles of a right triangle are complementary:  
$\text{Second angle} = 90^\circ - 37^\circ = 53^\circ$.

**Exercise 4.**  
The base angles share the remaining degrees equally:  
$\text{Base angle} = \frac{180^\circ - 50^\circ}{2} = \frac{130^\circ}{2} = 65^\circ \quad \text{each}$.

**Exercise 5.**  
The vertically opposite angle also measures $72^\circ$.  
The two adjacent supplementary angles each measure $180^\circ - 72^\circ = 108^\circ$.  
The four angles are $72^\circ$, $108^\circ$, $72^\circ$, and $108^\circ$.

**Exercise 6.**  
We know that $\widehat{D} + \widehat{E} + \widehat{F} = 180^\circ$, so $\widehat{D} + \widehat{E} = 180^\circ - 40^\circ = 140^\circ$.  
Since $\widehat{D} = 3 \times \widehat{E}$, we have $3 \times \widehat{E} + \widehat{E} = 4 \times \widehat{E} = 140^\circ$.  
Thus $\widehat{E} = 140^\circ \div 4 = 35^\circ$, and $\widehat{D} = 3 \times 35^\circ = 105^\circ$.  
Check: $105^\circ + 35^\circ + 40^\circ = 180^\circ$. ✔

**Exercise 7.**  
Let us compute the sum: $45^\circ + 65^\circ + 75^\circ = 185^\circ$.  
Since $185^\circ \neq 180^\circ$, such a triangle **cannot exist** in Euclidean geometry!

**Exercise 8.**  
1. No, because if one base angle were $100^\circ$, the second base angle would also be $100^\circ$, giving $100^\circ + 100^\circ = 200^\circ > 180^\circ$, which is impossible.

**Exercise 9.**  
The mason is using a scaled version of the Egyptian $(3, 4, 5)$ triangle multiplied by $20$:  
$3 \times 20 = 60\text{ cm}$, $4 \times 20 = 80\text{ cm}$.  
The hypotenuse must be $5 \times 20 = 100\text{ cm} = 1\text{ meter}$.  
If the distance between $A$ and $B$ is exactly $100\text{ cm}$, the corner is perfectly square.

**Exercise 10.**  
The transversal forms four angles of $64^\circ$ and four supplementary angles of $180^\circ - 64^\circ = 116^\circ$.

**Exercise 11.**  
Diagonal $[AC]$ splits quadrilateral $ABCD$ into two non-overlapping triangles, $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle ACD$. Each triangle has an angle sum of $180^\circ$. The sum of all interior angles of the quadrilateral is therefore:

**Exercise 12.**  
By considering the central regular pentagon and the five isosceles triangles that form the points, each tip angle of a regular star measures $36^\circ$.

We now know how to master straight lines, perpendiculars, and the sharp angles of polygons. But what happens when the lines begin to curve and sweep smoothly through space without a single corner?

In the dark corner of an ancient artisan's workshop, a heavy wooden disc spins smoothly upon an iron pivot: the **potter's wheel**. How can one measure the curved rim of a circle when no ruler can bend around it without slipping?
