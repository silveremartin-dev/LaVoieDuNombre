### 1. The Problem: The Baker's Barley Galette

The mouth-watering aroma of warm, golden bread drifts from the mouth of the communal clay-brick oven. The baker pulls out a magnificent flat barley galette onto a large wooden board—round like a golden disk, steaming and crispy.

Around the table, **four children** have bellies rumbling with anticipation.  
There is only one single galette for four hungry diners.

```
                  ┌──────────────────────┐
                  │   Only 1 galette     │
                  │   for 4 children     │
                  └──────────────────────┘
```

If the baker applied Euclidean division from the previous chapter:
$$1 = (4 \times 0) + 1$$
The quotient is 0, and the remainder is 1! The children would receive 0 galettes each, and the whole galette would sit on the table growing cold. That would be absurd and sorrowful!

The baker smiles, draws a sharp steel knife, and rests the blade right at the center of the galette:
1. *First straight cut:* the blade slices cleanly from edge to edge. Behold: **two identical half-galettes**.
2. *Second perpendicular cut:* the blade cuts across at a right angle. The galette is now divided into **four strictly equal quarters**.

```
                   Slice 1                   Slice 2
                  \         /               \         /
                   \       /                 \       /
             ───────[═════]───────     ───────[═════]───────
                   /       \                 /       \
                  /         \               /         \
                   Slice 3                   Slice 4
```

Each child holds out a hand and receives their share.  
How much did each child receive?  
They did not receive 0 galettes. Nor did they receive 1 whole galette.  
They received **one part coming from a whole cut into 4 equal pieces**.

To record this new kind of quantity, humans invented a two-story notation, separated by a horizontal bar:

$$\frac{1}{4}$$

This notation is read: **“one fourth”** (or **“one quarter”**).  
The word comes from the Latin *fractio*, meaning “the act of breaking into pieces”. You have just stepped into the world of **fractions**.

### 2. The Anatomy of a Fraction: The Sieve and the Harvest

A fraction is not an ordinary number resting on a line. It is an alliance between two integers playing two very distinct roles:

```
             ┌── The NUMERATOR (from Latin numerare = to count)
        3    │   It COUNTS how many parts are taken.
       ─── <─┤   
        4    │   The FRACTION BAR (the knife-cut of sharing)
             └── The DENOMINATOR (from Latin denominare = to name)
                 It NAMES the family of parts (into how many parts the unit is cut).
```

> **📐 Definition — Numerator and Denominator**  
> In a fraction written as $\frac{a}{b}$ (with $b \neq 0$):  
> - The **denominator** ($b$, at the bottom) indicates how many **equal** parts the whole unit has been divided into. It is the size of the “sieve”.  
> - The **numerator** ($a$, at the top) indicates the number of parts chosen, eaten, or shaded. It is the “harvest”.

#### How to Read a Fraction Aloud?
- The denominator is read using ordinal numbers: $\frac{1}{5}$ is “one fifth”, $\frac{3}{7}$ is “three sevenths”, $\frac{7}{10}$ is “seven tenths”.
- Pay attention to the **historical exceptions**:
  - $\frac{1}{2}$ is read “one **half**”;
  - $\frac{1}{3}$ is read “one **third**”;
  - $\frac{1}{4}$ is read “one **quarter**” (or “one fourth”).

#### The Denominator's Paradox: The Larger It Is, the Smaller the Piece!
Ask yourself this vital question:  
*Would you rather receive $\frac{1}{3}$ of an apple pie or $\frac{1}{8}$ of that exact same pie?*

A careless glance might suggest: *“8 is bigger than 3, so $\frac{1}{8}$ must be bigger!”*  
**That is a catastrophic mistake for a dessert lover!**

Think about the physical act of sharing:
- If you share a pie among **3 people** ($\frac{1}{3}$), you only need three slices: they are thick and hearty.
- If you must share the same pie among **8 people** ($\frac{1}{8}$), you have to make many more knife cuts: each slice becomes thin as paper!

```
     1/3 share (generous slice):        1/8 share (slender sliver):
              /=======\                         /===\
             (         )                       (     )
              \=======/                         \===/
```

$$\frac{1}{3} > \frac{1}{8}$$

> **⚠️ Pitfall Warning — The Denominator Rule**  
> For fractions sharing the **same numerator** (for instance 1 on top):  
> **The larger the denominator, the smaller the individual piece!**  
> $$\frac{1}{2} > \frac{1}{3} > \frac{1}{4} > \frac{1}{5} > \frac{1}{10} > \frac{1}{100}$$

> **🔍 True History — The Scribes of Ancient Egypt (The Rhind Papyrus)**  
> More than 3,500 years ago, the pharaohs' scribes almost exclusively recorded fractions with a **1** in the numerator ($\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{10}$). To express a quantity like $\frac{3}{4}$, they decomposed it into a sum of unit fractions: $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4}$. The famous **Rhind Papyrus** (preserved in the British Museum) contains dozens of division tables designed to fairly allocate bread and beer among the workers building the pyramids.

### 3. Comparing a Fraction to One: Smaller, Equal, or Greater?

Look at a box of 6 chocolates. The whole unit is the complete box ($6$ chocolates).

```
   [ o | o | o | o | o | o ]   <-- 1 full box = 6/6
```

1. **If you eat 4 chocolates:** you have eaten $\frac{4}{6}$ of the box.  
   The numerator ($4$) is smaller than the denominator ($6$).  
   You have eaten **less than one whole box**: $\frac{4}{6} < 1$.

2. **If you eat all 6 chocolates:** you have eaten $\frac{6}{6}$ of the box.  
   The numerator equals the denominator.  
   You have eaten **exactly 1 whole box**: $\frac{6}{6} = 1$.

3. **If you are ravenous and eat 11 chocolates** (by opening a second box):  
   You have eaten $\frac{11}{6}$ of a box.  
   The numerator ($11$) is larger than the denominator ($6$).  
   You have eaten **more than one whole box**: $\frac{11}{6} > 1$.

> **📐 Property — Position Relative to 1**  
> For any fraction $\frac{a}{b}$:  
> - If the numerator is strictly less than the denominator ($a < b$), then $\frac{a}{b} < 1$.  
> - If the numerator is equal to the denominator ($a = b$), then $\frac{a}{b} = 1$.  
> - If the numerator is strictly greater than the denominator ($a > b$), then $\frac{a}{b} > 1$.

> **Worked Example 1 — Comparing to 1**  
> Let us compare the following fractions to 1: $\frac{3}{8}$, $\frac{7}{7}$, $\frac{9}{4}$, $\frac{5}{6}$.  
> - For $\frac{3}{8}$: $3 < 8$, so $\frac{3}{8} < 1$.  
> - For $\frac{7}{7}$: $7 = 7$, so $\frac{7}{7} = 1$.  
> - For $\frac{9}{4}$: $9 > 4$, so $\frac{9}{4} > 1$.  
> - For $\frac{5}{6}$: $5 < 6$, so $\frac{5}{6} < 1$.

### 4. Decomposing a Fraction: The Bridge Between Division and Fractions

When a fraction is larger than 1, it hides complete whole units inside!  
Consider the fraction $\frac{7}{3}$ (seven thirds of a galette).  
Each whole galette contains 3 thirds ($\frac{3}{3} = 1$).  
How many whole galettes can we assemble with 7 thirds?

Recall Euclidean division of 7 by 3:
$$7 = (3 \times 2) + 1$$
In 7, there are **2 groups of 3**, with **1 left over**.  
This means that with 7 thirds, we can build **2 full galettes**, with **1 third remaining**!

$$\frac{7}{3} = \frac{3}{3} + \frac{3}{3} + \frac{1}{3} = 1 + 1 + \frac{1}{3} = 2 + \frac{1}{3}$$

```
   Galette 1 (3/3)        Galette 2 (3/3)        Galette 3 remainder (1/3)
      [///]                  [///]                  [/  ]
      1 whole        +       1 whole        +       1/3 remaining  =  2 + 1/3
```

> **🧭 Method — Writing a Fraction as “Whole Number + Fraction < 1”**  
> To decompose an improper fraction $\frac{a}{b}$:  
> 1. Carry out the Euclidean division of $a$ by $b$: find quotient $q$ and remainder $r$ ($a = b \times q + r$).  
> 2. Write directly:  
>    $$\frac{a}{b} = q + \frac{r}{b}$$  
> Since $r < b$, the fraction $\frac{r}{b}$ is guaranteed to be strictly less than 1!

> **Worked Example 2 — Decomposing an Improper Fraction**  
> Decompose $\frac{23}{5}$.  
> - Division of 23 by 5: $23 = (5 \times 4) + 3$ (quotient 4, remainder 3).  
> - We therefore have: $\frac{23}{5} = 4 + \frac{3}{5}$.  
> This shows that $\frac{23}{5}$ lies strictly between 4 and 5: $4 < \frac{23}{5} < 5$.

### 5. Placing Fractions on a Graduated Number Line

A graduated number line is a measuring path. To place fractions upon it:
1. Identify the unit interval between 0 and 1 (and between 1 and 2, 2 and 3, etc.).
2. Look at the denominator: it indicates the number of equal sub-intervals to create within each whole unit.
3. Count steps forward according to the numerator.

Let us draw a line where each unit is partitioned into **4 equal parts** (quarters):

```
   0         1/4       2/4       3/4        1        5/4       6/4       7/4        2
   |----.----|----.----|----.----|----.----|----.----|----.----|----.----|----.----|
   0/4                                     4/4                                  8/4
             ▲                             ▲                   ▲
          Point A                       Point B             Point C
```

- Point A is located 1 quarter from the start: it represents $\frac{1}{4}$.
- Point B is located right on 1: it represents $\frac{4}{4} = 1$.
- Point C is located 6 quarters from the start: it represents $\frac{6}{4} = 1 + \frac{2}{4}$.

> **Worked Example 3 — Reading and Placing on the Line**  
> *Question:* On a number line where each unit is divided into 5 equal parts (fifths), where does $\frac{8}{5}$ lie?  
> *Answer:* Start at 0 and move forward 8 tick marks of size $\frac{1}{5}$. Since $\frac{8}{5} = 1 + \frac{3}{5}$, the point is located between 1 and 2, exactly 3 ticks past 1.

### 6. Equivalent Fractions: The Secret of Paper Folding

Take a strip of white paper.  
Fold it exactly in half. Color one half blue.  
You have colored **1 part out of 2**: that is $\frac{1}{2}$.

Now, without cutting anything or adding any ink, fold the folded strip in half once more.  
Unfold it.  
What do you notice?  
The whole strip is now divided into **4 equal sections**.  
And the blue part now contains **2 sections out of 4**!

```
   Strip folded in 2:              Strip folded in 4:
   ┌──────────────┬──────────────┐ ┌──────┬──────┬──────┬──────┐
   │    Blue      │    White     │ │ Blue │ Blue │White │White │
   │   ( 1/2 )    │   ( 1/2 )    │ │(1/4) │(1/4) │      │      │
   └──────────────┴──────────────┘ └──────┴──────┴──────┴──────┘
           1 part out of 2                 2 parts out of 4!
```

Did the amount of blue ink increase? No!  
Is the strip any longer? No!  
Only the fineness of the division changed. You multiplied the total number of slices by 2 (at the bottom), but you also multiplied the number of blue slices by 2 (at the top):

$$\frac{1}{2} = \frac{1 \times 2}{2 \times 2} = \frac{2}{4}$$

If you fold the strip in half a third time, you obtain:
$$\frac{2}{4} = \frac{2 \times 2}{4 \times 2} = \frac{4}{8}$$

All these fractions describe the exact **same quantity of matter**. We call them **equivalent fractions** (or equal fractions).

> **📐 The Golden Rule of Equivalent Fractions**  
> You do not alter the value of a fraction if you **multiply** (or **divide**) both its numerator and denominator by the **same non-zero whole number**:  
> $$\frac{a}{b} = \frac{a \times k}{b \times k} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{a}{b} = \frac{a \div k}{b \div k}$$

> **Worked Example 4 — Generating Equivalent Fractions**  
> - Find a fraction equal to $\frac{3}{5}$ with a denominator of 20:  
>   To go from 5 to 20, we multiply by 4 ($5 \times 4 = 20$).  
>   We must also multiply the top by 4: $3 \times 4 = 12$.  
>   $$\frac{3}{5} = \frac{3 \times 4}{5 \times 4} = \frac{12}{20}$$  
> - Simplify $\frac{6}{8}$:  
>   6 and 8 are both divisible by 2:  
>   $$\frac{6}{8} = \frac{6 \div 2}{8 \div 2} = \frac{3}{4}$$

### 7. Adding and Subtracting Fractions with the Same Denominator

Imagine a large cake cut into 8 equal slices (eighths).  
- At lunch, Leo eats 2 slices: he eats $\frac{2}{8}$ of the cake.  
- At afternoon snack, his sister Sarah eats 3 slices: she eats $\frac{3}{8}$ of the same cake.

How much cake did they eat altogether?  
They ate $2 \text{ slices} + 3 \text{ slices} = 5 \text{ slices}$ of size “eighth”.  
Therefore, they ate $\frac{5}{8}$ of the cake!

$$\frac{2}{8} + \frac{3}{8} = \frac{2 + 3}{8} = \frac{5}{8}$$

```
   Cake cut into 8 slices:
   [ Leo | Leo | Sarah | Sarah | Sarah |     |     |     ]
   <--- 2/8 ---> <------- 3/8 ------->
   <---------------- 5/8 ------------>
```

> **📐 Rule — Addition and Subtraction with the Same Denominator**  
> To add (or subtract) two fractions that have the **same denominator**:  
> 1. **Keep the same denominator** (do not touch the bottom!).  
> 2. **Add (or subtract) the numerators** (compute only on the top).  
>  
> $$\frac{a}{c} + \frac{b}{c} = \frac{a + b}{c} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{a}{c} - \frac{b}{c} = \frac{a - b}{c}$$

> **⚠️ Pitfall Warning — The Forbidden Mistake**  
> Never add denominators together!  
> $$\frac{1}{4} + \frac{2}{4} \neq \frac{1+2}{4+4} = \frac{3}{8} \quad \text{TOTALLY FALSE!}$$  
> If you combine 1 quarter of a pie and 2 quarters of a pie, you get 3 quarters of a pie ($\frac{3}{4}$), not 3 eighths! The size of the slice does not shrink when you place several on the same plate.

> **Worked Example 5 — Simple Calculations**  
> - $A = \frac{3}{7} + \frac{2}{7} = \frac{3+2}{7} = \frac{5}{7}$  
> - $B = \frac{9}{10} - \frac{4}{10} = \frac{9-4}{10} = \frac{5}{10} = \frac{1}{2}$  
> - $C = 1 - \frac{3}{5}$:  
>   Rewrite the integer 1 as a fraction with denominator 5: $1 = \frac{5}{5}$.  
>   $$C = \frac{5}{5} - \frac{3}{5} = \frac{5-3}{5} = \frac{2}{5}$$

### What You Need to Remember

1. **A fraction** $\frac{a}{b}$ represents an equal division: the bottom ($b$, denominator) gives the number of equal parts in the whole; the top ($a$, numerator) counts the parts taken.
2. **Vocabulary:** $\frac{1}{2}$ (one half), $\frac{1}{3}$ (one third), $\frac{1}{4}$ (one quarter / fourth), $\frac{a}{b}$ ($a$ $b$-ths).
3. **Comparison to 1:**  
   - $a < b \implies \frac{a}{b} < 1$  
   - $a = b \implies \frac{a}{b} = 1$  
   - $a > b \implies \frac{a}{b} > 1$  
4. **Decomposition:** For $\frac{a}{b} > 1$, perform Euclidean division $a = b \times q + r$, yielding $\frac{a}{b} = q + \frac{r}{b}$ (a whole number plus a proper fraction).
5. **Equivalent fractions:** Multiplying or dividing both top and bottom by the same non-zero number leaves the fraction's value unchanged ($\frac{1}{2} = \frac{2}{4} = \frac{5}{10}$).
6. **Addition / Subtraction:** With identical denominators, compute only across the numerators: $\frac{a}{c} + \frac{b}{c} = \frac{a+b}{c}$.

### The Question that Opens What Lies Ahead

Thanks to fractions, you now know how to cut any galette into 3, 4, 7, or 12 equal parts.  
Yet Renaissance merchants and engineers faced a persistent, frustrating headache:  
To compare $\frac{3}{7}$ and $\frac{4}{9}$, or to add mixed thirds, fourths, and fifths, they constantly had to search for awkward common denominators.

In 1585, a brilliant Flemish engineer and mathematician named **Simon Stevin** cried out:  
*“Why burden ourselves with all these disparate denominators? Our number system already counts in bundles of 10: units, tens, hundreds. Let us simply extend this very scale below the number 1: tenths, hundredths, thousandths!”*

To separate the realm of whole items from the realm of decimal crumbs, all that was needed was a single magic punctuation mark: **the decimal point** (or comma).  
Prepare to discover decimal numbers in **Chapter 11: The Tenths of Simon Stevin**!
