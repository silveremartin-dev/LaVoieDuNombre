### 1. The Problem: The Tower of Babel of Old Regime Measurements

Before 1789, France was not united in its measurements: it was a bewildering labyrinth!  
Every province, every town, and often every feudal estate had its own measuring units:
- In Paris, cloth was measured using the **Paris ell** (*aune*, about 1.18 meters). But in Rouen, the ell was shorter, and in Lyon, it was longer!
- To measure land, surveyors used the **King's Foot** (about 32.5 cm), but the foot in Bordeaux was 35.7 cm.
- To weigh grain, people used the **pound** (*livre*), but more than 50 different kinds of pounds existed across France!
- For liquids, the Saint-Denis **pint** was twice as large as the Paris pint.

Historians estimate that France possessed over **800 different names for measures** and nearly **250,000 different ways of calculating them**!

```
   Before 1789:
   In Paris: 1 foot = 32.5 cm
   In Bordeaux: 1 foot = 35.7 cm
   In Strasbourg: 1 foot = 28.9 cm
   ===> Honest trade between cities was practically impossible!
```

Dishonest merchants took advantage of this confusion to cheat customers; peasants were ruined by feudal lords who secretly enlarged grain bushels to collect heavier taxes; nobody could agree.

In 1789, the French Revolution erupted. In their registers of grievances (*cahiers de doléances*), citizens demanded with one voice:  
*“One king, one law, one weight, and one measure!”*

### 2. Measuring the Earth: The Adventure of the Universal Meter

The scientists of the French Academy of Sciences (Lavoisier, Condorcet, Lagrange, Laplace) refused to base the new unit on the length of a king’s foot or an emperor’s arm: kings die, and human limbs vary from person to person.  
They sought a standard belonging to all humankind, drawn from nature itself: **planet Earth**!

> **🔍 True History — The Epic Journey of Delambre and Méchain (1792–1798)**  
> In 1792, in the midst of revolutionary turmoil and the threat of war, two French astronomers set out to measure the Earth:  
> - **Jean-Baptiste Delambre** traveled south from Dunkirk.  
> - **Pierre Méchain** traveled north from Barcelona.  
> For six arduous years, across rugged mountains, violent storms, and arrests by suspicious revolutionary guards who mistook their surveying theodolites for spy equipment, they measured the distance between Dunkirk and Barcelona using triangulation.  
>  
> From these measurements, scientists determined the exact total distance along the Earth's meridian arc from the North Pole to the Equator.  
> They divided this immense distance into **ten million equal parts**.  
> This ten-millionth part of the Earth's meridian quadrant became the new standard of length for humanity: **the Meter** (from the Greek *metron*, meaning “measure”).

```
               NORTH POLE
                  / \
                 /   \   
                |  D  |  <-- Dunkirk
                |  B  |  <-- Barcelona
                 \   /
                  \ /
                EQUATOR
   Distance North Pole to Equator divided by 10,000,000 = EXACTLY 1 METER!
```

The motto of this new universal standard was engraved upon the first official commemorative medals:  
**“For all times, for all peoples.”**

### 3. The Universal Chart: The Magic of Greek and Latin Prefixes

The scholars had a second stroke of genius: apply Simon Stevin’s decimal place-value system to every physical measurement.  
Gone were the clumsy systems where 1 foot was 12 inches and 1 inch was 12 lines!  
From now on, **everything scales by tens**.

To name the units, they devised a brilliant linguistic convention:
- For units **larger** than the base unit, they borrowed words from **Greek** (the language of giants):  
  - **Deca-** (from Greek *deka*) = $\times 10$  
  - **Hecto-** (from Greek *hekaton*) = $\times 100$  
  - **Kilo-** (from Greek *khilioi*) = $\times 1\,000$  
- For units **smaller** than the base unit, they borrowed words from **Latin** (the language of division):  
  - **Deci-** (from Latin *decimus*) = $\div 10$ ($\frac{1}{10}$)  
  - **Centi-** (from Latin *centesimus*) = $\div 100$ ($\frac{1}{100}$)  
  - **Milli-** (from Latin *millesimus*) = $\div 1\,000$ ($\frac{1}{1\,000}$)  

```
             GREEK PREFIXES (× 10, × 100, × 1 000)  ──┐
             kilo-    hecto-    deca-                 │
                                                      ├──> BASE UNIT (m, g, L)
             LATIN PREFIXES (÷ 10, ÷ 100, ÷ 1 000)  ──┘
             deci-    centi-    milli-
```

This exact same chart structure works **identically** for lengths, masses, and capacities!

#### 1. Lengths (Base unit: the meter $m$)

| Kilometer ($km$) | Hectometer ($hm$) | Decameter ($dam$) | Meter ($m$) | Decimeter ($dm$) | Centimeter ($cm$) | Millimeter ($mm$) |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| $1\,000\text{ m}$ | $100\text{ m}$ | $10\text{ m}$ | **$1\text{ m}$** | $0.1\text{ m}$ | $0.01\text{ m}$ | $0.001\text{ m}$ |

#### 2. Capacities (Base unit: the liter $L$)

| Hectoliter ($hL$) | Decaliter ($daL$) | Liter ($L$) | Deciliter ($dL$) | Centiliter ($cL$) | Milliliter ($mL$) |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| $100\text{ L}$ | $10\text{ L}$ | **$1\text{ L}$** | $0.1\text{ L}$ | $0.01\text{ L}$ | $0.001\text{ L}$ |

*(Note: the kiloliter is rarely used in daily conversation; people usually refer to cubic meters).*

#### 3. Masses (Base units: the gram $g$ and the kilogram $kg$)

| Metric Ton ($t$) | Quintal ($q$) | — | Kilogram ($kg$) | Hectogram ($hg$) | Decagram ($dag$) | Gram ($g$) | Decigram ($dg$) | Centigram ($cg$) | Milligram ($mg$) |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| $1\,000\text{ kg}$ | $100\text{ kg}$ | | **$1\text{ kg}$** | $100\text{ g}$ | $10\text{ g}$ | **$1\text{ g}$** | $0.1\text{ g}$ | $0.01\text{ g}$ | $0.001\text{ g}$ |

> **⚠️ Pitfall Warning — The Conversion Table Rules**  
> 1. Place only **one digit per column** in the conversion chart.  
> 2. The **units digit** of the starting number must always sit in the column of the given unit!  
> 3. *Example:* To enter $34.8\text{ m}$, the units digit is 4. Place 4 in the $m$ column, 3 in $dam$, and 8 in $dm$.  
>    To read this in centimeters: append zeros out to the $cm$ column $\to 3\,480\text{ cm}$.

> **Worked Example 1 — Direct Conversions**  
> - Convert $2.5\text{ km}$ to meters: $2.5\text{ km} = 2\,500\text{ m}$.  
> - Convert $750\text{ mL}$ to liters: $750\text{ mL} = 0.750\text{ L} = 0.75\text{ L}$.  
> - Convert $3.4\text{ metric tons}$ to kilograms: $1\text{ t} = 1\,000\text{ kg} \implies 3.4\text{ t} = 3\,400\text{ kg}$.

### 4. Length vs. Area: The String and the Tile (1D vs. 2D)

Here is one of the most common confusions in all of geometry: the difference between **perimeter** and **area**.

```
    PERIMETER = 1 DIMENSION (1D)           AREA = 2 DIMENSIONS (2D)
    The fence running around the edge.     The grass covering the pasture.
    Measured in meters (m) or cm.          Measured in square meters (m²) or cm².
    
    ┌──────────────────────┐               ┌██████████████████████┐
    │                      │               │██████████████████████│
    │     The boundary     │               │     The covered      │
    │                      │               │       interior       │
    └──────────────────────┘               └██████████████████████┘
```

- **Perimeter:** the total distance around the outside edge of a shape. Imagine an ant walking all the way around a field, or unrolling a spool of fence wire along the boundary. It is a **1-dimensional** measure (pure length).
- **Area (surface):** the total amount of 2D space enclosed within the boundary. Imagine laying down square floor tiles, or painting a wall. It is a **2-dimensional** measure ($\text{width} \times \text{length}$).

---

### 5. Calculating the Perimeter of a Polygon

To find the perimeter of any polygon, simply **add together the lengths of all its sides**.

> **📐 Common Perimeter Formulas**  
> - **Any polygon:** $\mathcal{P} = \text{sum of all outer sides}$.  
> - **Rectangle with length $L$ and width $w$:**  
>   $$\mathcal{P} = L + w + L + w = 2 \times (L + w)$$  
> - **Square with side length $s$:**  
>   $$\mathcal{P} = 4 \times s$$

> **Worked Example 2 — Perimeter Calculations**  
> - A rectangle measures $8\text{ cm}$ long and $5\text{ cm}$ wide.  
>   $\mathcal{P} = 2 \times (8 + 5) = 2 \times 13 = 26\text{ cm}$.  
> - A square has side length $6\text{ m}$.  
>   $\mathcal{P} = 4 \times 6 = 24\text{ m}$.

### 6. Measuring Area: From Grids to Square Centimeters ($\text{cm}^2$)

To measure surface area, we pave the surface with standard square tiles.

```
   1 unit tile = 1 cm on each side
   Its area is exactly 1 square centimeter, written 1 cm².
   
      1 cm
     ┌───┐
     │ 1 │ 1 cm      Area = 1 cm²
     └───┘
```

Consider a rectangle $5\text{ cm}$ long and $3\text{ cm}$ wide.  
Cover it completely with $1\text{ cm}^2$ tiles:

```
     1 cm  2 cm  3 cm  4 cm  5 cm
    ┌─────┬─────┬─────┬─────┬─────┐
    │  1  │  2  │  3  │  4  │  5  │  1st row of 5 tiles
    ├─────┼─────┼─────┼─────┼─────┤
    │  6  │  7  │  8  │  9  │ 10  │  2nd row of 5 tiles
    ├─────┼─────┼─────┼─────┼─────┤
    │ 11  │ 12  │ 13  │ 14  │ 15  │  3rd row of 5 tiles
    └─────┴─────┴─────┴─────┴─────┘
```

To count the total number of square tiles, there is no need to count them one by one:  
We have **3 rows of 5 tiles each**!  
$$\text{Area} = 5 \times 3 = 15\text{ tiles of } 1\text{ cm}^2 = 15\text{ cm}^2$$

> **📐 Common Area Formulas**  
> - **Area of a rectangle:**  
>   $$\mathcal{A} = \text{Length} \times \text{Width} = L \times w$$  
> - **Area of a square:**  
>   $$\mathcal{A} = \text{side} \times \text{side} = s \times s$$

> **Worked Example 3 — Never Confuse Perimeter and Area!**  
> Consider a square with side length $s = 4\text{ cm}$.  
> - Its perimeter: $\mathcal{P} = 4 \times 4 = 16\text{ cm}$. (Length of string around the edges).  
> - Its area: $\mathcal{A} = 4 \times 4 = 16\text{ cm}^2$. (Number of square carpet tiles).  
> Both numbers happen to be 16, but their units describe completely different things: one is in **centimeters**, the other in **square centimeters**!  
> Now look at a square with side length $5\text{ cm}$:  
> - $\mathcal{P} = 4 \times 5 = 20\text{ cm}$.  
> - $\mathcal{A} = 5 \times 5 = 25\text{ cm}^2$.  
> Here, $25 \neq 20$!

### 7. Angles: Acute, Right, or Obtuse with a Set Square

When two straight lines meet at a point (the vertex), they form an opening called an **angle**.  
To inspect this opening, the fundamental reference tool of students and carpenters is the **set square** (or drafting triangle).

```
    ACUTE ANGLE                RIGHT ANGLE                 OBTUSE ANGLE
    (Sharper than set square)  (Matches set square: 90°)   (Wider than set square)
          /                          |                            \
         /                           |                             \
        /_____                      |_____                        \_____
```

1. **Right angle ($90^\circ$):** the angle of a perfect sheet of paper, the corner of a square room, or the corner of a set square. Its two rays are perpendicular.
2. **Acute angle:** a sharp, narrow angle tighter than a right angle (opening strictly between $0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$).
3. **Obtuse angle:** an open, wide angle broader than a right angle (opening strictly between $90^\circ$ and $180^\circ$).

> **🧭 Method — Checking Angles with a Set Square**  
> 1. Place the vertex of the set square's right angle directly onto the angle's vertex.  
> 2. Align one straight edge of the set square along one arm of the angle.  
> 3. Observe the other arm:  
>    - If it hides beneath the body of the set square: the angle is **acute** (smaller than $90^\circ$).  
>    - If it traces exactly along the second edge: the angle is **right** ($90^\circ$).  
>    - If it spreads outward beyond the set square: the angle is **obtuse** (greater than $90^\circ$).

### What You Need to Remember

1. **The metric system** is anchored on the **meter** ($m$), defined during the French Revolution as a decimal fraction of planet Earth.
2. **Prefixes:**  
   - Kilo ($1\,000$), Hecto ($100$), Deca ($10$) to multiply;  
   - Deci ($\frac{1}{10}$), Centi ($\frac{1}{100}$), Milli ($\frac{1}{1\,000}$) to divide.
3. **Masses:** $1\text{ kg} = 1\,000\text{ g}$ and $1\text{ metric ton (t)} = 1\,000\text{ kg}$.
4. **Perimeter (1D):** Total boundary length.  
   - Rectangle: $\mathcal{P} = 2 \times (L + w)$.  
   - Square: $\mathcal{P} = 4 \times s$.
5. **Area (2D):** Total enclosed surface.  
   - Rectangle: $\mathcal{A} = L \times w$.  
   - Square: $\mathcal{A} = s \times s$.
6. **Angles:**  
   - Acute ($< 90^\circ$);  
   - Right ($= 90^\circ$, corner of set square);  
   - Obtuse ($> 90^\circ$).

### Solutions

**1.**  
a) $4\text{ m} = \mathbf{400\text{ cm}}$;  
b) $3.5\text{ km} = \mathbf{3\,500\text{ m}}$;  
c) $250\text{ mm} = \mathbf{25\text{ cm}}$;  
d) $12\text{ dm} = \mathbf{1.2\text{ m}}$.  

**2.**  
a) $5\text{ kg} = \mathbf{5\,000\text{ g}}$;  
b) $2.8\text{ t} = \mathbf{2\,800\text{ kg}}$;  
c) $750\text{ g} = \mathbf{0.75\text{ kg}}$;  
d) $4\,200\text{ mg} = \mathbf{4.2\text{ g}}$.  

**3.**  
a) $2\text{ L} = \mathbf{200\text{ cL}}$;  
b) $1.5\text{ L} = \mathbf{1\,500\text{ mL}}$;  
c) $33\text{ cL} = \mathbf{0.33\text{ L}}$;  
d) $25\text{ dL} = \mathbf{2.5\text{ L}}$.  

**4.** $\mathcal{P} = 2 \times (7 + 4) = 2 \times 11 = \mathbf{22\text{ cm}}$.  
**5.** $\mathcal{A} = s \times s = 6 \times 6 = \mathbf{36\text{ cm}^2}$.  
**6.** Sharper than a right angle = **acute angle**. Broader than a right angle = **obtuse angle**.  
**7.**  
a) $\mathcal{P} = 2 \times (45 + 20) = 2 \times 65 = \mathbf{130\text{ m}}$.  
b) Fencing cost: $130 \times 8 = \mathbf{\$1\,040}$.  
c) Field area: $\mathcal{A} = 45 \times 20 = \mathbf{900\text{ m}^2}$.  

**8.**  
a) $1.2\text{ m} = \mathbf{120\text{ cm}}$.  
b) $\mathcal{P} = 2 \times (120 + 40) = 2 \times 160 = \mathbf{320\text{ cm}} = \mathbf{3.2\text{ m}}$.  
c) $\mathcal{A} = 120 \times 40 = \mathbf{4\,800\text{ cm}^2}$.  

**9.**  
a) Side length: $36 \div 4 = \mathbf{9\text{ cm}}$.  
b) Square area: $9 \times 9 = \mathbf{81\text{ cm}^2}$.  

**10.** Flour used: $350 + 420 = 770\text{ g}$.  
Since $1\text{ kg} = 1\,000\text{ g}$, there remains: $1\,000 - 770 = \mathbf{230\text{ g}}$ of flour.  

**11.** $1.5\text{ L} = 150\text{ cL}$.  
Number of glasses: $150 \div 25 = \mathbf{6\text{ full glasses}}$.  

**12.**  
a) Perimeter of A: $2 \times (6 + 4) = 2 \times 10 = \mathbf{20\text{ cm}}$.  
b) Perimeter of B: $2 \times (8 + 3) = 2 \times 11 = \mathbf{22\text{ cm}}$.  
c) **No!** Two shapes can encompass the exact same surface area (here $24\text{ cm}^2$) while having perimeters of different lengths. The more elongated and narrow a rectangle is, the larger its perimeter for a given area.  

**13.**  
a) The walkway adds $1\text{ m}$ on each side:  
- New length: $10 + 1 + 1 = \mathbf{12\text{ m}}$.  
- New width: $5 + 1 + 1 = \mathbf{7\text{ m}}$.  
b) Total combined area: $12 \times 7 = \mathbf{84\text{ m}^2}$.  
c) Pool area alone: $10 \times 5 = 50\text{ m}^2$.  
Walkway area alone: $84 - 50 = \mathbf{34\text{ m}^2}$.  

**14.**  
Mass of cement bags: $80 \times 35 = 2\,800\text{ kg} = 2.8\text{ metric tons}$.  
Total mass of loaded truck: $2.4 + 2.8 = \mathbf{5.2\text{ metric tons}}$.  

**15.**  
Let a square have side length $s = 2\text{ cm}$:  
$\mathcal{P}_1 = 4 \times 2 = 8\text{ cm}$ and $\mathcal{A}_1 = 2 \times 2 = 4\text{ cm}^2$.  
If we double the side ($s = 4\text{ cm}$):  
$\mathcal{P}_2 = 4 \times 4 = 16\text{ cm}$ and $\mathcal{A}_2 = 4 \times 4 = 16\text{ cm}^2$.  
a) Perimeter is multiplied by **2** ($16 = 8 \times 2$).  
b) Area is multiplied by **4** ($16 = 4 \times 4$, because $2 \times 2 = 4$)! A square twice as wide contains 4 smaller squares inside.
