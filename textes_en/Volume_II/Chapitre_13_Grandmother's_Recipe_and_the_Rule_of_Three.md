### 1. The Problem: The Birthday Feast and the Cake for 4

Thomas’s birthday is just around the corner. To treat his friends, his grandmother pulls out her treasured recipe notebook, stained with cocoa and dog-eared from years of love.  
She turns to her legendary pear chocolate fudge cake:

```
    PEAR FUDGE CAKE RECIPE (For 4 people):
    - 200 g dark chocolate
    - 100 g flour
    - 4 fresh eggs
    - 80 g sugar
    - 2 ripe pears
```

Thomas jumps with delight:  
*“Grandma, this is wonderful! But there will be **6 people** at the table for my birthday: my parents, you, my two cousins, and me! And tomorrow, we’re having a second party with **10 classmates**!”*

Grandmother smiles:  
*“If we simply toss in 6 eggs at random and 300 grams of sugar, our cake will turn out cloyingly sweet or terribly dry. We must respect **the right proportions**!”*

```
   For 4 people  ──> 200 g chocolate
   For 6 people  ──> ??? g chocolate
   For 10 people ──> ??? g chocolate
```

How can we calculate the exact amount of chocolate for 6 people without ruining Grandma's recipe?

Thomas takes a sheet of scrap paper and thinks it through:
- For 4 people, we need 200 g of chocolate.
- Therefore, for half as many (**2 people**), we need half as much: $200 \div 2 = 100\text{ g}$.
- And **6 people** is simply 4 people plus 2 people!  
We can just add the corresponding amounts together:
$$200\text{ g} + 100\text{ g} = 300\text{ g of chocolate!}$$

Thomas has just used, without realizing it, one of the most powerful tools in everyday mathematics: **proportionality**.

### 2. What Is Proportionality? The Doubling Test

In nature and daily life, many quantities are linked together. But they are not all linked in the same way!

> **📐 Definition — Two Proportional Quantities**  
> Two quantities are **proportional** when the values of one are obtained by **always multiplying the values of the other by the exact same constant number**.  
> This implies that:  
> - If one quantity becomes **2 times larger**, the other becomes **2 times larger**.  
> - If one quantity becomes **3 times larger**, the other becomes **3 times larger**.  
> - If one quantity becomes **2 times smaller** (halved), the other becomes **2 times smaller**.

#### The Pitfall: Not Everything in Life Is Proportional!

Let us put three everyday situations to the **“doubling test”**:

1. **The Price of Apples:**  
   $1\text{ kg}$ of apples costs $\$3$.  
   $2\text{ kg}$ of apples cost $\$6$ ($2 \times 3$).  
   $4\text{ kg}$ of apples cost $\$12$ ($4 \times 3$).  
   $\to$ **This is a true proportional relationship!**

2. **A Child's Age and Height:**  
   At age 5, Tom was $1.10\text{ m}$ tall.  
   At age 10 (his age has doubled: $5 \times 2 = 10$), Tom is about $1.40\text{ m}$ tall.  
   Is he twice as tall as $1.10\text{ m}$, which would be $2.20\text{ m}$? Absolutely not!  
   And at age 20, will he stand $4.40\text{ m}$ tall? That would be a terrifying giant!  
   $\to$ **Height is NOT proportional to age!** Human bodies grow quickly in early childhood, slow down, and stop growing altogether in adulthood.

3. **A Cinema Membership:**  
   An annual membership card costs $\$10$, and then each movie ticket costs $\$5$.  
   - For 1 movie: $\$10 + \$5 = \$15$.  
   - For 2 movies: $\$10 + (2 \times \$5) = \$20$.  
   The number of movies doubled (from 1 to 2), but the total cost did not double ($\$20 \neq 2 \times \$15 = \$30$).  
   $\to$ Because of the fixed starting fee, this is **NOT direct proportionality**.

### 3. The Proportionality Table: Three Calculation Methods

To solve problems cleanly without getting tangled up, we arrange our numbers into a two-row table:

```
   Quantity 1 (e.g., number of notebooks)  │  2  │  4  │  6  │ 10  │
   ────────────────────────────────────────┼─────┼─────┼─────┼─────┤
   Quantity 2 (e.g., cost in dollars)      │  5  │ 10  │ 15  │ 25  │
```

There are **three reliable methods** for filling in missing entries in this table. Always pick the one that makes the numbers simplest to compute!

#### Method 1: Operating Across Columns (Multiplication and Addition)
In a proportionality table, you are allowed to:
- Multiply or divide an entire column by any number.
- Add (or subtract) two columns together!

```
                      × 2                  +
             ┌──────────────────┐   ┌─────────────┐
             │                  ▼   │             ▼
   Notebooks │   2   │   4   │  6   │  10  (4 + 6 = 10)
   ──────────┼───────┼───────┼──────┼─────────────────
   Cost ($)  │   5   │  10   │ 15   │  25  (10 + 15 = 25)
             │                  ▲   │             ▲
             └──────────────────┘   └─────────────┘
                      × 2                  +
```

#### Method 2: Finding the Unit Rate (How Much for 1?)
This is the common-sense method favored by generations of shoppers:  
*“First find the value for **1 single item**, then multiply by the desired number of items!”*

> **Worked Example 1 — The Unit Rate Method**  
> *Problem:* 3 identical pens cost $\$4.50$. How much do 7 of these pens cost?  
> - **Step 1: Find the price of 1 pen.**  
>   $\$4.50 \div 3 = \$1.50$. (One pen costs $\$1.50$).  
> - **Step 2: Calculate the cost for 7 pens.**  
>   $7 \times \$1.50 = \$10.50$.  
> 7 pens cost **$\$10.50$**.

#### Method 3: The Constant of Proportionality (The Vertical Bridge)
This is the multiplier that takes you directly from the top row to the bottom row in one step.

```
   Number of cakes       │   3   │   5   │   8   │
   ──────────────────────┼───────┼───────┼───────┤   × 4  (Constant)
   Number of strawberries│  12   │  20   │  32   │
```

To find this multiplier, divide any number in the second row by its corresponding partner in the first row:
$$\text{Constant} = \frac{12}{3} = 4$$
Every cake requires 4 strawberries. For any order, simply **multiply by 4**! And to work backwards from strawberries to cakes, divide by 4.

### 4. The Legendary “Rule of Three” Explained

For centuries across European schools, pupils were taught **“the rule of three”**. Why that name? Because you are given **three known numbers**, and your goal is to uncover the **fourth**!

Consider the setup:
$$\begin{array}{ccc}
a & \longrightarrow & b \\
c & \longrightarrow & ?
\end{array}$$

*Example:* If $4$ meters of fabric cost $\$28$, how much do $9$ meters cost?
- The three known numbers are: $4$, $28$, and $9$.
- The unknown number is labeled $?$.

How did ancient merchants solve this in a single concise line?  
They combined finding the unit rate and multiplying:
1. The price of 1 meter is: $\frac{28}{4} = \$7$.  
2. The price of 9 meters is: $9 \times 7 = \$63$.  

In a single expression:
$$? = \frac{28 \times 9}{4} = \frac{252}{4} = \$63$$

```
                4 m  ───────────>  $28
                     \         /
                      \   ×   /
                       \     /
                9 m  ───\───/───>  ? = (9 × 28) ÷ 4 = $63
```

Multiply the two numbers located along the diagonal pair, then divide by the third remaining number! This is also known as **cross-multiplication**.

### 5. Two Classic Applications: Speed and Map Scales

#### 1. Traveling at Constant Speed: Kilometers per Hour ($km/h$)
When a train or car travels at a steady speed of **$80\text{ km/h}$** (80 kilometers per hour), it covers $80\text{ km}$ in exactly $1\text{ hour}$.  
Distance covered is directly proportional to elapsed travel time!

Let us build the table:

| Time ($h$) | $1\text{ h}$ | $2\text{ h}$ | $3\text{ h}$ | $30\text{ min}$ ($\frac{1}{2}\text{ h}$) | $15\text{ min}$ ($\frac{1}{4}\text{ h}$) |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Distance ($km$) | **$80$** | $160$ | $240$ | $40$ | $20$ |

- In $2\text{ h}$: twice as much time $\to 2 \times 80 = 160\text{ km}$.  
- In $30\text{ min}$ (half an hour): half the distance $\to 80 \div 2 = 40\text{ km}$.  
- In $15\text{ min}$ (a quarter of an hour): $80 \div 4 = 20\text{ km}$.

> **Worked Example 2 — Road Trip**  
> A car drives at an average speed of $90\text{ km/h}$.  
> What distance does it cover in $2\text{ h } 30\text{ min}$?  
> - In $2\text{ h}$: $2 \times 90 = 180\text{ km}$.  
> - In $30\text{ min}$ (half-hour): $90 \div 2 = 45\text{ km}$.  
> - Total distance: $180 + 45 = \mathbf{225\text{ km}}$.

#### 2. Geographic Maps and Architectural Models: Scales
On a road map or city floorplan, the real world is scaled down proportionally.  
If the scale says:  
**$1\text{ cm on the map} = 100\text{ m in the real world}$**  
This means all real-world distances are divided by $10\,000$ (since $100\text{ m} = 10\,000\text{ cm}$).

| On the map ($cm$) | $1\text{ cm}$ | $2\text{ cm}$ | $5\text{ cm}$ | $10\text{ cm}$ |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| In reality | $100\text{ m}$ | $200\text{ m}$ | $500\text{ m}$ | $1\,000\text{ m}$ ($1\text{ km}$) |

> **Worked Example 3 — Forest Hike**  
> On a hiking trail map where $1\text{ cm}$ represents $250\text{ m}$, two mountain villages are linked by a trail measuring $8\text{ cm}$ on the map.  
> What is the actual distance between the two villages?  
> - $8 \times 250\text{ m} = 2\,000\text{ m} = \mathbf{2\text{ km}}$.

### What You Need to Remember

1. **Two quantities are proportional** if you can move from one to the other by multiplying by a fixed constant value (the **constant of proportionality**).
2. **Properties of proportionality tables:**  
   - You can multiply or divide any column by a constant.  
   - You can add or subtract columns together.
3. **The unit rate method:** Find the value for 1 unit (by division), then multiply to find the desired target quantity.
4. **The rule of three:** To solve $\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{?}$, calculate $? = \frac{b \times c}{a}$.
5. **Constant speed:** Distance is proportional to elapsed time ($\text{distance} = \text{speed} \times \text{time}$).
6. **Map scale:** Distances drawn on paper are proportional to physical distances on the ground.
