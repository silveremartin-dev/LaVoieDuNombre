### 1. The Problem: The Nightmare of the Merchants of Bruges

The year is 1585 in the bustling Flemish city of Bruges. The canals are crowded with barges laden with grain, spices, barrels of beer, and rolls of fine Flemish cloth.

On the quays, merchants and tax collectors are tearing their hair out.  
To calculate the total cost of a shipment of wheat, a merchant must add:
- $14$ whole florins,
- plus $\frac{3}{7}$ of a florin for cart transport,
- plus $\frac{5}{12}$ of a florin for the canal bridge toll,
- minus $\frac{1}{6}$ of a florin for a trade discount!

Every trader uses fractions with completely different denominators: thirds, sevenths, twelfths, sixteenths. They constantly have to find common denominators, convert fractions, simplify… Calculations drag on for hours, errors multiply, and bitter arguments erupt across the marketplace.

One man observes this chaos with calm clarity. His name is **Simon Stevin** (1548–1620). He is an engineer, mathematician, and quartermaster general of the Dutch army.

> **💡 Discovery & History — Simon Stevin (1548–1620)**  
> 
> *“Why make complicated what can be simple? We count by tens, hundreds, and thousands. Let us do the exact same thing BELOW the unit!”*

Stevin asks an illuminating question:  
*“When we write 111, the first 1 represents 100, the second represents 10, and the third represents 1. Each rank is 10 times smaller than the one before it. Why stop abruptly at 1? Let us continue! The next rank will be 10 times smaller than 1, and the one after that 10 times smaller still!”*

In 1585, Stevin publishes a slim 36-page booklet written in Flemish entitled *De Thiende* (“The Tenth”), translated into French as *La Disme* and English as *The Art of Tenths* or *The Decimal*.  
This marks the official birth of **decimal numbers**.

### 2. From Sliced Cake to Place Value: Decimal Fractions

To understand the journey below the number 1, imagine a long measuring strip of length 1 meter.

```
   0                                                             1 meter
   |-------------------------------------------------------------|
```

1. **The Tenths:**  
   Divide this meter into **10 equal sections**. Each piece is a decimal fraction:  
   $$\frac{1}{10} \text{ of a meter (one tenth of a meter, or } 1 \text{ decimeter)}$$  
   It takes 10 tenths to make 1 whole unit: $\frac{10}{10} = 1$.

2. **The Hundredths:**  
   Take one of these tenths and divide it into **10 tiny sub-pieces**.  
   Since there were 10 tenths, the whole meter is now partitioned into $10 \times 10 = 100$ small pieces!  
   Each tiny piece is called a **hundredth**:  
   $$\frac{1}{100} \text{ of a meter (one hundredth of a meter, or } 1 \text{ centimeter)}$$  
   It takes 10 hundredths to build 1 tenth ($\frac{10}{100} = \frac{1}{10}$).  
   And it takes 100 hundredths to form 1 whole unit ($\frac{100}{100} = 1$).

3. **The Thousandths:**  
   Divide each hundredth into 10 even finer dust-like specks. The entire unit is now cut into $100 \times 10 = 1\,000$ parts!  
   Each speck is called a **thousandth**:  
   $$\frac{1}{1\,000} \text{ of a meter (one thousandth of a meter, or } 1 \text{ millimeter)}$$

> **📐 Definition — Decimal Fraction**  
> A **decimal fraction** is any fraction whose denominator is $10, 100, 1\,000, 10\,000\dots$ (a power of 10).

### 3. The Decimal Point: The Border Guard of Place Value

How can we write these fractions without drawing two-story fraction bars every time?  
Look at our grand place-value chart extended to the right:

| ... | Hundreds ($h$) | Tens ($t$) | Units ($u$) | . | Tenths ($\frac{1}{10}$) | Hundredths ($\frac{1}{100}$) | Thousandths ($\frac{1}{1\,000}$) |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| | $100$ | $10$ | $1$ | **.** | $0.1$ | $0.01$ | $0.001$ |
| | | | **3** | **.** | **4** | **7** | |

Look at this number: **$3.47$**.  
It consists of two distinct territories:

```
                  3    .    4 7
                 ───       ─────
                  │           │
             Whole part   Decimal part
        (3 whole units)   (4 tenths and 7 hundredths)
```

> **📐 Definition — Decimal Number, Whole Part, and Decimal Part**  
> A **decimal number** is a number that can be expressed with a finite number of digits after the decimal point.  
> - The **whole-number part** is located to the left of the decimal point (units, tens, hundreds...).  
> - The **decimal part** is located to the right of the decimal point (tenths, hundredths, thousandths...).  
> - The **decimal point** (or comma) is the border marking where the single units column ends.

#### How to Read $3.47$ Aloud?
- In casual speech: people often say “three point four seven”.
- But the precise mathematical reading is: “**3 units and 47 hundredths**” or “**3 units, 4 tenths, and 7 hundredths**”.

$$3.47 = 3 + \frac{4}{10} + \frac{7}{100} = 3 + \frac{47}{100} = \frac{347}{100}$$

> **💡 Did You Know?**  
> Simon Stevin did not yet use our modern decimal point! He drew tiny circled numbers above the digits: $3\text{ \textcircled{0} } 4\text{ \textcircled{1} } 7\text{ \textcircled{2} }$ to designate whole units, tenths, and hundredths.  
> It was the Scottish mathematician **John Napier** (the inventor of logarithms) who popularized the decimal point and comma in 1617. Today, English-speaking countries use a dot ($3.47$), while France and many European nations use a decimal comma ($3{,}47$). The mathematical meaning is identical!

### 4. The Great Pitfall: Why $3.2$ Is Greater Than $3.18$!

This is the number-one pitfall in elementary school mathematics—one that almost every student stumbles into at least once!

Look at these two numbers:
$$3.2 \quad \text{and} \quad 3.18$$
An untrained eye glances at the decimal part and thinks:  
*“18 is bigger than 2, so 3.18 must be bigger than 3.2!”*  
**That is completely wrong!**

Why? Because you are comparing slices of completely different sizes!  
- In $3.2$, the 2 represents **2 tenths** of a cake (2 large, thick slices).  
- In $3.18$, the 1 represents **1 tenth** and the 8 represents **8 tiny hundredths**.

To compare fairly, we must express both in the same unit of measure (hundredths).  
Add a **trailing zero** at the end of $3.2$:
$$3.2 = 3.20 \quad \text{(3 whole units and 20 hundredths)}$$

Now compare:
- $3.20$ is 3 cakes and **20 hundredths**.  
- $3.18$ is 3 cakes and **18 hundredths**.  

Since $20 > 18$, it is crystal clear that:
$$\mathbf{3.2 > 3.18}$$

```
   3.20:  [ Cake 1 ] [ Cake 2 ] [ Cake 3 ]  +  [ 20 hundredths ]  <-- LARGER!
   3.18:  [ Cake 1 ] [ Cake 2 ] [ Cake 3 ]  +  [ 18 hundredths ]
```

> **🧭 Method — Comparing Two Decimal Numbers Without Error**  
> 1. First compare the **whole-number part**:  
>    $7.1 > 5.99$ because $7 > 5$. (No need to even look past the decimal point!).  
> 2. If the whole parts are equal, compare the **tenths digit**:  
>    In $3.\mathbf{2}$ and $3.\mathbf{1}8$, the tenths digits are $2$ versus $1$. Since $2 > 1$, it follows that $3.2 > 3.18$.  
> 3. If the tenths are equal, compare the **hundredths**, and so on.  
> *Foolproof tip:* Pad the numbers with trailing zeros on the right so both have the same number of decimal places!

> **⚠️ Pitfall Warning — Meaningful vs. Superfluous Zeros**  
> - **Superfluous zeros:** You can freely add or remove zeros at the very end of the decimal part without changing the number's value:  
>   $$4.5 = 4.50 = 4.500$$  
> - **Indispensable zeros:** A zero trapped between the decimal point and another non-zero digit can NEVER be removed:  
>   $$4.05 \neq 4.5$$  
>   $4.05$ is 4 units and 5 hundredths. $4.5$ is 4 units and 5 tenths (50 hundredths)! That is 10 times more crumb!

### 5. Interpolation and Bounding: The Infinite World Between Two Numbers

With whole numbers, between 3 and 4, there is **nothing**. No integer can slip between them.  
With decimals, the world becomes infinitely rich!

Look at a ruler between 3 and 4:
```
   3           3.2         3.4         3.6         3.8          4
   |-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|
        3.1         3.3         3.5         3.7         3.9
```
Between 3 and 4, we have interpolated 9 numbers at the tenths level: $3.1, 3.2, 3.3\dots$

And what happens between $3.1$ and $3.2$?  
Write them in hundredths: $3.10$ and $3.20$.  
Between $3.10$ and $3.20$, we can slip:
$$3.11 \quad ; \quad 3.12 \quad ; \quad 3.15 \quad ; \quad 3.19$$
And between $3.14$ and $3.15$? We can write $3.140$ and $3.150$, and insert thousandths like $3.145$!

> **💡 Did You Know?**  
> Between any two decimal numbers, no matter how close they seem, you can ALWAYS find another decimal number (in fact, infinitely many!). There is never an empty gap that cannot be filled by zooming in further.

> **Worked Example 1 — Bounding and Interpolation**  
> - *Bound 4.72 between consecutive whole numbers:* $4 < 4.72 < 5$.  
> - *Bound 4.72 to the nearest tenth:* $4.7 < 4.72 < 4.8$ (since $4.70 < 4.72 < 4.80$).  
> - *Insert a decimal between 6.3 and 6.4:*  
>   Think of $6.30$ and $6.40$. We can choose $6.35$ (or $6.31$ or $6.38$).

### 6. Column Addition and Subtraction: The Shirt Buttons

To add or subtract decimal numbers, the golden rule is simple and absolute:  
**You must vertically align the decimal points directly under one another.**

Think of the decimal point as the line of buttons on a shirt: if one button is off by a single hole, the entire shirt sits crooked!

#### Worked Example: Calculate $43.7 + 8.56$

```
           t   u . tenths  hundredths
           1   1                 <-- carry digits
           4   3 .    7      0   <-- we pad with a 0 to balance columns
       +       8 .    5      6
       ───────────────────────
           5   2 .    2      6
```

1. Align the decimal points vertically.
2. Add a trailing zero to $43.7$ so both numbers have two decimal digits: $43.70$.
3. Add column by column from right to left:
   - Hundredths: $0 + 6 = 6$.
   - Tenths: $7 + 5 = 12 \implies$ write 2, carry 1 to the units column.
   - **Immediately place the decimal point in the answer, perfectly aligned!**
   - Units: $1 \text{ (carry)} + 3 + 8 = 12 \implies$ write 2, carry 1 to the tens.
   - Tens: $1 \text{ (carry)} + 4 = 5$.
4. **Result:** $52.26$.

#### Worked Example: Calculate $35.2 - 7.64$

```
           t   u . tenths  hundredths
           2  14     11     10   <-- borrowing / regrouping
           3   5 .    2      0   <-- this zero is MANDATORY!
       -       7 .    6      4
       ───────────────────────
           2   7 .    5      6
```

> **⚠️ Pitfall Warning — The Mandatory Zero in Subtraction**  
> In $35.2 - 7.64$, you cannot simply say “nothing minus 4 is 4”! That is a grave error.  
> You must write $35.2\mathbf{0}$.  
> Then calculate: 0 hundredths minus 4 hundredths cannot be done directly $\implies$ regroup 1 tenth into 10 hundredths, and $10 - 4 = 6$!

### 7. Multiplying by 10, 100, and 1,000: The Great Place-Value Shift

What happens when you multiply a decimal number by 10?  
Remember: each unit becomes a ten, each tenth becomes a unit, and each hundredth becomes a tenth!  
Every digit jumps one place-value column to the left.  
On paper, this creates the visual appearance that **the decimal point shifts to the right**.

```
    × 10    ──> Decimal point shifts 1 place to the right:    3.45 × 10 = 34.5
    × 100   ──> Decimal point shifts 2 places to the right:   3.45 × 100 = 345
    × 1 000 ──> Decimal point shifts 3 places to the right:   3.45 × 1 000 = 3 450
```

> **Worked Example 2 — Mental Fluency**  
> - $0.6 \times 10 = 6$  
> - $2.38 \times 100 = 238$  
> - $4.7 \times 100 = 470$ (shift one place to make 47, then append a zero for the second place!)  
> - $0.005 \times 1\,000 = 5$

---

### What You Need to Remember

1. **A decimal number** consists of a whole-number part and a decimal part separated by a decimal point.
2. **Place value:**  
   - 1st digit after the point = **tenths** ($\frac{1}{10} = 0.1$);  
   - 2nd digit = **hundredths** ($\frac{1}{100} = 0.01$);  
   - 3rd digit = **thousandths** ($\frac{1}{1\,000} = 0.001$).
3. **Comparing decimals:** Always look at the whole-number part first. If equal, compare tenths, then hundredths. Pad with trailing zeros to level columns ($3.2 = 3.20 > 3.18$).
4. **Column addition/subtraction:** Line up decimal points vertically without fail. Remember to pad top numbers with zeros in subtraction when needed ($5 - 2.3 = 5.0 - 2.3$).
5. **Multiplying by 10, 100, 1,000:** Shift the decimal point 1, 2, or 3 places to the right, adding zeros as necessary.

### The Question that Opens What Lies Ahead

Thanks to Simon Stevin’s decimals, humanity gained a flawless system for measuring continuous magnitudes.  
Yet until the end of the 18th century, every city and province in France used entirely different units: a league in Paris was not a league in Lyon, a pint varied from town to town, and the King's Foot was not the size of anyone else's shoe!

In 1789, the French Revolution broke out. Citizens and scholars resolved to unite the nation—and soon the entire world—around a single universal standard of measure drawn directly from the Earth itself: **the Meter**.  
And for all physical magnitudes, from length and mass to liquid volume, they embraced the elegance of the decimal system!
