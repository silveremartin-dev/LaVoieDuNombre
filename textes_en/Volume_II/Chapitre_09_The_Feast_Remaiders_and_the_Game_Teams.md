### 1. The Problem: The Fourteen Sturgeons of the Great River

Morning mist floats over the slow waters of the great river. The village’s heavy fishing dugout pulls up against the wooden logs of the landing dock. The fishermen leap into the cool water and heave a dripping net onto the bank: they have caught **14 large golden sturgeons**, glistening in the morning sun.

Four fishing families gather in the central square around a massive flat stone table. Each family has sent its representative. The 14 fish are lined up across the table, their eyes bright and scales sparkling.

The village elder steps forward with a solemn gaze:  
*“These fish must feed our children. But take heed: every family must receive exactly the same share. Not one bone more, not one scale less! Otherwise, jealousy will tear our village apart.”*

```
   On the stone table: 14 fish
   ><>  ><>  ><>  ><>  ><>  ><>  ><>  ><>  ><>  ><>  ><>  ><>  ><>  ><>
   
   4 families are waiting: [Family A]  [Family B]  [Family C]  [Family D]
```

You are tasked with carrying out the division. How can you proceed so that no injustice is done?

You do not plunge into complicated formulas. You place your hands on the fish and perform a universal gesture—the same one children use when dealing cards in a game: **you distribute one fish at a time, round after round**.

```
   Round 1:   Family A [><>]   Family B [><>]   Family C [><>]   Family D [><>]
              (4 fish distributed, 10 remain on the table)

   Round 2:   Family A [><>]   Family B [><>]   Family C [><>]   Family D [><>]
              (8 fish distributed in total, 6 remain on the table)

   Round 3:   Family A [><>]   Family B [><>]   Family C [><>]   Family D [><>]
              (12 fish distributed in total, 2 fish remain on the table!)
```

Look at the table after the third round:
- Each family has received **3 large fish**. Everyone is treated completely equally.
- But in the center of the stone, **2 whole fish** remain.

Can you deal a fourth round?  
No! If you give one fish to Family A and one to Family B, Families C and D will protest loudly: *“Why them and not us? That is unjust!”*

Because these fish are fresh and must be salted whole to be preserved, they cannot be sliced into pieces today. Sharing whole objects has reached its limit.

You have just carried out a fundamental operation in mathematics: **Euclidean division** (integer division with remainder), named in honor of the ancient Greek scholar **Euclid**, who taught in Alexandria more than 2,300 years ago.

### 2. The Four Actors of Sharing: The Anatomy of Division

Let us give precise names to the four quantities that played a part in this distribution:

```
         14          =       (4        ×        3)       +        2
          ↑                   ↑                 ↑                 ↑
     The Dividend        The Divisor       The Quotient      The Remainder
    (total bounty)     (number of shares) (share per party)  (what is left)
```

> **📐 Definition — The Four Roles of Euclidean Division**  
> When we share an integer $a$ into $b$ equal parts:  
> 1. The **dividend** ($a$) is the total quantity of items to be shared.  
> 2. The **divisor** ($b$) is the number of equal shares to form (it can never be 0!).  
> 3. The **quotient** ($q$) is the fair share received by each recipient.  
> 4. The **remainder** ($r$) is what is left unshared at the end.

These four numbers are bound together by an essential equation that every mathematician keeps in mind:

$$a = (b \times q) + r$$

We read aloud: “**Dividend equals divisor times quotient plus remainder.**”

#### The Inviolable Golden Rule: The Condition on the Remainder

Imagine a clumsy distributor who says:  
*“I shared the 14 fish among the 4 families. I gave 2 fish to each, and I have 6 fish left on the table!”*

Look at the corresponding equality:
$$14 = (4 \times 2) + 6$$
In pure arithmetic, the sum is correct: $4 \times 2 = 8$, and $8 + 6 = 14$. Yet this distribution is **wrong**!  
Why? Because with the 6 fish left on the table, there were easily enough fish to give one more to each of the 4 families ($6 \ge 4$). The distributor simply stopped too early out of laziness!

> **⚠️ Pitfall Warning — The Inviolable Golden Rule**  
> In any Euclidean division, **the remainder must ALWAYS be strictly less than the divisor**:  
> $$r < b$$  
> And naturally, the remainder cannot be negative: $0 \le r < b$.  
>  
> If you divide by 4, the only possible remainders are: **0, 1, 2, or 3**.  
> The moment your remainder is equal to or greater than 4, your quotient is too small: you can still distribute more!

When the remainder is **0**, we say that **the division has no remainder** (it divides evenly). The dividend is then an exact multiple of the divisor (as we explored in Chapter 8).

> **Worked Example 1 — Identifying the Four Actors**  
> In the division of 37 by 5:  
> - In the 5-times table, find the largest multiple less than or equal to 37: $5 \times 7 = 35$, and $5 \times 8 = 40$ (too large).  
> - Thus: $37 = (5 \times 7) + 2$.  
> - The dividend is $a = 37$.  
> - The divisor is $b = 5$.  
> - The quotient is $q = 7$.  
> - The remainder is $r = 2$.  
> - Checking the golden rule: $2 < 5$. The condition is perfectly satisfied!

### 3. Two Meanings for One Operation: Sharing or Grouping?

Division actually answers two very distinct types of real-world questions. Learning to tell them apart is essential for solving word problems clearly.

```
                    ┌── MEANING 1: EQUAL SHARING (How many per person?)
                    │   We know the number of shares; we look for the share size.
    DIVISION ───────┤
                    └── MEANING 2: GROUPING / CAPACITY (How many groups?)
                        We know the size of each group; we look for the number of groups.
```

#### Meaning 1: Fair Sharing (Dealing Cards)
- **What is known:** the total number of items ($a$) and the number of recipients ($b$).
- **What we seek:** the size of the share received by each person ($q$).
- *Example:* 35 candies shared among 4 children. Each child receives 8 candies, and 3 remain in the bag.

#### Meaning 2: Grouping or Capacity (Packing Boxes)
- **What is known:** the total number of items ($a$) and the capacity of one box ($b$).
- **What we seek:** how many full boxes can be packed ($q$).
- *Example:* A baker bakes 35 chocolate eggs. He packs them into boxes of 4 eggs. How many full boxes can he seal? He can seal 8 full boxes, leaving 3 eggs left over.

> **Worked Example 2 — Sharing or Grouping?**  
> *Situation A:* A teacher has 28 notebooks for 7 students. How many notebooks does each student receive?  
> **Answer:** This is **sharing** (we seek each student's share). $28 = 7 \times 4 + 0$. Each student receives 4 notebooks.  
>  
> *Situation B:* A florist has 28 roses. She arranges them into bouquets of 6 roses. How many bouquets can she make?  
> **Answer:** This is **grouping** (we package by groups of 6). $28 = 6 \times 4 + 4$. She makes 4 complete bouquets, and 4 roses remain.

```
     Dividend ─────┐   ┌─── Divisor
                   │   │
                   └───┼───────────
                       │    Quotient
     Remainder ────────┘
```

Why this layout? Because it enables us to divide the number **chunk by chunk**, starting from the left (the highest place-value: hundreds, then tens, then units). It is the only standard arithmetic algorithm that works from left to right!

#### Detailed Case Study: Calculating $75 \div 3$

We wish to share 75 gold coins among 3 treasure chests.  
75 is **7 tens and 5 units**.

```
       7 5  |  3
            |-------
            |  
```

**Step 1: Divide the tens.**
- Place your finger on the 7. In 7 tens, how many times does 3 fit?
- In the 3-times table: $3 \times 2 = 6 \le 7$. We can put **2** tens into each chest.
- Write 2 in the quotient.
- Subtract the distributed tens: $7 - 6 = 1$. There is **1 ten unshared**.

```
       7 5  |  3
     - 6    |-------
     ----   |  2
       1    |
```

**Step 2: Bring down the next digit.**
- 1 ten alone cannot be shared among 3 chests.
- Exchange it for 10 units and bring down the 5 beside it. This yields **15 units**.

```
       7 5  |  3
     - 6    |-------
     ----   |  2 5
       1 5  |
```

**Step 3: Divide the units.**
- In 15 units, how many times does 3 fit?
- In the 3-times table: $3 \times 5 = 15$ exactly!
- Write 5 in the quotient, to the right of 2.
- Subtract the distributed units: $15 - 15 = 0$.

```
       7 5  |  3
     - 6    |-------
     ----   |  2 5
       1 5  |
     - 1 5  |
     -----  |
         0  |
```

**Result:** The quotient is **25**, and the remainder is **0**.  
Each chest receives 25 gold coins. The division divides evenly!

#### A Richer Case: Calculating $473 \div 4$

Let us see what happens when digits have non-zero remainders at each stage.

```
       4 7 3  |  4
              |-------
              |
```

1. **Hundreds:** In 4, how many times 4? Exactly **1** time ($1 \times 4 = 4$). Remainder: $4 - 4 = 0$.
2. **Bring down 7 (tens):** In 7, how many times 4? Exactly **1** time ($1 \times 4 = 4$). Remainder: $7 - 4 = 3$.
3. **Bring down 3 (units):** We get 33. In 33, how many times 4? $4 \times 8 = 32$. It fits **8** times. Remainder: $33 - 32 = 1$.

```
       4 7 3  |  4
     - 4      |-------
     ----     |  1 1 8
       0 7    |
     -   4    |
     -----    |
         3 3  |
       - 3 2  |
       -----  |
           1  |
```

**Result:** $473 = (4 \times 118) + 1$.  
The quotient is 118, and the remainder is 1. The condition $1 < 4$ is fully respected!

> **🧭 Method — The 4 Magic Moves of Long Division**  
> For each column in division, we repeat the same four moves:  
> 1. **D**ivide (how many times does the divisor fit?)  
> 2. **M**ultiply (calculate what was just distributed: $q \times b$)  
> 3. **S**ubtract (find the remainder)  
> 4. **B**ring down (drop down the next digit to form a new working number)  
>  
> Remember the magic acronym: **D - M - S - B** (*Divide, Multiply, Subtract, Bring down*)!

### 5. Checking Your Division: The Foolproof Verification

In mathematics, you never finish a calculation without verifying your work. To check a Euclidean division, we perform the inverse operation: **we retrace our steps using multiplication**.

> **Worked Example 3 — Verifying $473 = 4 \times 118 + 1$**  
> 1. Multiply the quotient by the divisor:  
>    $$118 \times 4 = (100 \times 4) + (10 \times 4) + (8 \times 4) = 400 + 40 + 32 = 472$$  
> 2. Add the remainder:  
>    $$472 + 1 = 473$$  
> 3. Do we get back the original dividend? **Yes, exactly 473!**  
> The calculation is certified error-free.

> **⚠️ Pitfall Warning — The Trap of Zero in the Quotient**  
> This is the most common mistake made by fourth-grade students!  
> Let us divide $615 \div 3$:  
> - In 6, how many times 3? 2 times ($2 \times 3 = 6$). Remainder 0.  
> - Bring down the 1. In 1, how many times 3? **It fits 0 times!**  
> - Many students forget to write **0** in the quotient and immediately bring down the 5. They end up with a quotient of 25 instead of **205**!  
> But $3 \times 25 = 75$, which is nowhere near 615! With the zero in place, $3 \times 205 = 615$.  
> **Whenever you bring down a digit, you MUST write a digit in the quotient, even if that digit is zero!**

### 6. Additional Worked Examples

> **Worked Example 4 — Bags of Marbles**  
> A child has 146 marbles. He wants to pack them into bags of 6 marbles each.  
> *Question:* How many full bags will he get, and how many marbles will remain?  
> - In 1: 6 does not fit. Take 14.  
> - In 14, how many times 6? $6 \times 2 = 12$. Remainder: $14 - 12 = 2$.  
> - Bring down the 6: we have 26.  
> - In 26, how many times 6? $6 \times 4 = 24$. Remainder: $26 - 24 = 2$.  
> - Euclidean form: $146 = (6 \times 24) + 2$.  
> **Conclusion:** He fills **24 full bags** and has **2 marbles left over**.

> **Worked Example 5 — Minibuses for Field Trips**  
> 83 people are going on a field trip. Each minibus can carry at most 9 passengers.  
> *Question:* What is the minimum number of minibuses required to transport everyone?  
> - Perform the Euclidean division of 83 by 9:  
>   $83 = (9 \times 9) + 2$ (since $9 \times 9 = 81$).  
> - The quotient is 9, the remainder is 2.  
> - 9 minibuses will transport $9 \times 9 = 81$ people.  
> - But 2 people would be left standing on the curb! Will we leave them behind? Of course not!  
> **Conclusion:** We must order **10 minibuses** (9 full minibuses and 1 minibus with only 2 passengers).  
> *This example shows why we must always consider the concrete meaning of a problem: sometimes the answer is the quotient, and sometimes it is quotient + 1!*

### 5bis. The Challenge of Great Merchants: Two-Digit Divisors

In fourth and fifth grade, real-world problems often require dividing by a two-digit number, such as distributing $7,845 \text{ liters of oil}$ into barrels holding $23 \text{ liters}$.

Faced with a divisor like 23, no one has memorized the “23-times table”! Seasoned calculators use a foolproof tool: **the scratchpad mini-table**.

#### Calculating $7,845 \div 23$ Step by Step:

1. **The scratchpad mini-table:**  
   Quickly calculate the first few multiples of 23:  
   - $23 \times 1 = 23$  
   - $23 \times 2 = 46$  
   - $23 \times 3 = 69$  
   - $23 \times 4 = 92$  

2. **Set up long division:**  
   - In 7, 23 does not fit. Take **78**.  
   - In our mini-table: $23 \times 3 = 69 \le 78$. Place **3** in the quotient.  
   - Subtraction: $78 - 69 = 9$.  
   - Bring down 4: we get **94**.  
   - In our mini-table: $23 \times 4 = 92 \le 94$. Place **4** in the quotient.  
   - Subtraction: $94 - 92 = 2$.  
   - Bring down 5: we get **25**.  
   - In 25, how many times 23? It fits **1** time ($1 \times 23 = 23$). Place **1** in the quotient.  
   - Final subtraction: $25 - 23 = 2$.

```
       7 8 4 5  |  2 3
     - 6 9      |───────
     ─────      |  3 4 1
         9 4    |
       - 9 2    |
       ─────    |
           2 5  |
         - 2 3  |
         ─────  |
             2  |
```

**Result:** $7,845 = (23 \times 341) + 2$ with $2 < 23$. Everything is validated!

### 🛠️ The Boundary Case Laboratory: What Happens with ZERO in Division?

*(This section is isolated to analyze extreme edge cases without disrupting your standard calculations.)*

#### Case A: Zero Divided by a Number ($0 \div 5$)
- **The scenario:** You have a completely empty basket (0 apples), and 5 children sit at the table. How many apples does each child receive?  
- Each child receives **0 apples**.  
- Verification: $5 \times 0 = 0$. Everything works smoothly!  
- **Rule:** Zero divided by any non-zero number always equals **zero**:  
  $$\frac{0}{b} = 0 \quad (\text{for all } b \ne 0)$$

#### Case B: The Absolute Prohibition: Dividing by ZERO ($5 \div 0$)
- Imagine trying to calculate $5 \div 0 = q$.  
- If such a quotient existed, our verification formula would require:  
  $$0 \times q = 5$$  
- But we established that **zero multiplied by any number is ALWAYS zero** ($0 \times q = 0$)! It is strictly impossible for it to equal 5.  
- No number in the entire universe can satisfy this equation.  
- **Absolute Rule of Mathematics:** **WE NEVER DIVIDE BY ZERO.** It is a mathematical black hole that destroys the meaning of equality.

### What You Need to Remember

1. **Euclidean division** is used to divide a whole quantity into equal parts or to count how many full groups can be formed.
2. **The 4 actors:**  
   $$\text{Dividend} = (\text{Divisor} \times \text{Quotient}) + \text{Remainder}$$  
   $$a = (b \times q) + r$$
3. **The mandatory golden rule:** The remainder must always be strictly less than the divisor:  
   $$0 \le r < b$$
4. **Long division layout:** We process digits from left to right following the cycle: *Divide $\to$ Multiply $\to$ Subtract $\to$ Bring down*.
5. **Zero in the quotient:** Whenever you bring down a digit and cannot form a group, you must write a `0` in the quotient before bringing down the next digit.
6. **Verification:** Always verify using $(b \times q) + r$.
